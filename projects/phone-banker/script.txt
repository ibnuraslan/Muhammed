The Phone That Killed a Banker
Narration: 1,010 words across three chapters. Estimated spoken runtime: 8 min 5 s at 125 wpm.

Look at the plastic on this telephone. There's a faint stain.
The phone belongs to a Moscow banker. By the time investigators examine it, he and his secretary are both dead.
The substance on the handset is still affecting people who come into the office. Among them are eight police officers.
When investigators search the bank, they find the clue on the one thing sitting in plain sight on his desk. His telephone.

## Chapter One · Two Hospitals
On August first, 1995, Ivan Kivelidi is at work in Moscow. He runs Rosbusinessbank. That afternoon, he makes several phone calls from the office, according to reporting at the time. His brother comes by and finds him looking unwell. Kivelidi has a history of kidney trouble. There's already a familiar explanation for why he might suddenly need a doctor.
By the time he reaches the hospital, he's in a coma.
The next evening, an ambulance takes another person from the same bank to a different hospital. Her name is Zara Ismailova. She's Kivelidi's secretary. She's unconscious when she arrives. At six the next morning, she dies. Kivelidi dies the following day.
The pathologist assigned to examine Zara is a doctor named Iosif Laskavy. He later remembers opening her file and seeing two possible diagnoses, each followed by a question mark. Epilepsy. A problem with blood flow to the brain. Neither, he says, fits what he sees in the chart. Around the same time, he hears on the radio that Kivelidi has died. He holds off on the autopsy.
Later, forensic experts compare the records from the two hospitals. Both patients had constricted pupils. And in blood samples from both, the activity of an enzyme called cholinesterase had dropped almost to zero.
When a nerve tells a muscle to move, it releases a chemical signal. An enzyme breaks down the chemical so the signal can end. Block the enzyme and the signal keeps acting. Muscles can twitch, then stop working. Eventually, a person can lose the ability to breathe.
Back at the bank, investigators find a faint mark on the handset of a white desk phone. The case files describe residue beneath a small rubber plug in the handset.
It's Kivelidi's phone.
How Zara came into contact with it is less clear. One account says she may have wiped the handset. The exact moment she was exposed remains uncertain. But the phone is still a hazard days later. Among those affected, the files list a cleaner and officers who inspect the room.
At Kivelidi's memorial in Moscow City Hall, the people who come to pay their respects include Mikhail Gorbachev and Russia's prime minister. At the bank, the investigation comes down to a much shorter list. Who had been inside that office before Kivelidi fell ill?

## Chapter Two · The Man in the Office
One name stands out. Vladimir Khutsishvili is a business partner of Kivelidi's and sits on the bank's board. He visits the office that day. Investigators believe he spends time alone inside. They also find a potential source of friction. Kivelidi plans to issue new shares in the bank, which could reduce Khutsishvili's stake.
Police detain him that autumn. Thirty days later, they let him go. They have a visitor and a possible motive. They still need to show how he could have obtained the poison.
The lab work points investigators toward a class of chemicals that includes some of the most toxic nerve agents ever made. And as the investigation develops, the trail leads to a closed town in Russia's Saratov region.
In Shikhany, Leonid Rink works at a state research institute that develops chemical weapons. Years later, in testimony published by the Russian newspaper Novaya Gazeta, Rink describes a handover near Moscow's Belorussky railway station. A man he'd met through an acquaintance had asked him for poison, supposedly for a dog. Rink says he later supplied a different substance and was paid for it. In court he puts the payment at fifteen hundred dollars. He says the men knew where his daughter and his mother lived and that he gave in after they threatened his family.
Investigators believe that a batch of eight or nine glass ampoules was made for sale. Prosecutors trace an ampoule through intermediaries to Khutsishvili. In their account, he takes it to the bank and puts the poison on the handset while Kivelidi is out of the room.
By December 1999, prosecutors are looking for Khutsishvili again. He's gone abroad. Nearly seven years pass before he returns to Moscow. In June 2006, police arrest him outside an apartment building on Yefremova Street.

## Chapter Three · The Poison Without a Name
When the case goes to trial, the hearings are closed. Prosecutors argue the evidence contains sensitive information about the poison. Khutsishvili denies killing Kivelidi. His defense says the office door was open and people could see inside. It disputes that he had the opportunity to do what prosecutors describe.
On December twenty-fourth, 2007, the court sentences him to nine years. It finds him guilty of intentionally killing Kivelidi and of causing Zara Ismailova's death through negligence. He later wins early release. The conviction stands.
The court reaches a verdict without publicly naming the compound. Then, in 2018, a poisoning in England brings reporters back to the old case files.
British scientists identify a nerve agent from the Novichok family in the attack on Sergei Skripal and his daughter, Yulia. The British public inquiry later describes how the poison was placed on the handle of Skripal's front door. In Moscow, the object had been an office phone.
Novaya Gazeta publishes material from the Russian investigation, including an analysis of residue from the handset. Vladimir Uglev, who helped develop Soviet nerve agents, tells reporters that one of those agents was used on Kivelidi. Other specialists dispute what can be determined from the published analysis. Rink gives a new account, too. He tells the news outlet The Bell that he handed over rat poison in transactions monitored by the security services. That account conflicts with his earlier testimony.
The evidence photograph is still there. It shows a white handset and a faint mark on the plastic. The court decided who put the poison there. The files that later became public leave two questions open. Exactly what was on that phone? And how many of the other ampoules ever made it back into official hands?
