They Paid $30 for Radioactive Scrap
Narration: 1,080 words, four chapters. Estimated spoken runtime: 8 min 38 s at 125 wpm.

In December 1998, two brothers in Istanbul pay about thirty dollars for a pair of old metal containers. They break them open to sell the metal. Within days, people who helped take them apart start vomiting. So do some of the people who just stood nearby.
The family suspects the lead inside the containers. They bury it.
The thing that's making them sick stays in the yard.

## Chapter One · Across the Street
The brothers, Naki and İlyas Ilgaz, live in İkitelli, in western Istanbul. The containers come from a building that has just changed hands. The new owners are clearing it out. The brothers load both packages onto a truck and take them home.
On December tenth, the family starts taking them apart on a patch of open ground across from a relative's house. They strip away the outer cases and wooden crates. Then they pull the shielding plug and a sliding assembly out of one container. One man reaches into the opening to see what's inside.
Bad weather interrupts the work. They load everything back on the truck and roll it off into a yard beside the family home. The yard is open to the street. There are houses right next to it.
On December thirteenth, they go back to work. They open the second container and use a torch and an excavator to peel away the steel shells. Relatives watch as the lead inside is exposed.
By late afternoon, the people doing the work start feeling sick. Others who were nearby develop similar symptoms. At a local clinic, food poisoning and lead poisoning are both suspected. The patients get fluids. When the vomiting subsides, they go home.
A few days later, the man who reached inside notices that two fingertips are turning red.
Near the end of December, the scrap is loaded onto a truck by hand and with a shovel, then taken to a larger yard. By early January, much of that pile has already gone to a smelting works east of Istanbul. The capsule is still buried in the scrap at the second yard.

## Chapter Two · The Mark on the Container
On January eighth, two of the men go to a larger hospital. Nearly four weeks have passed since people first fell ill. They're still weak. Some of the people who worked on the containers have started bleeding from their gums.
A doctor named Çağlar Canpolat asks what they've been working with. The men describe the lead containers. Canpolat considers why a container would be lined with so much lead in the first place. Then the son of one of the patients mentions a mark he saw on the container. Three blades around a central circle. A radiation warning.
Canpolat calls the local nuclear research center. By three that afternoon, specialists are at the entrance to the second scrapyard. They're already getting high readings. The workers are evacuated. Police seal off the area.
The containers were built to carry cobalt-sixty for cancer treatment. Inside a radiotherapy machine, a source like this sends a beam of gamma rays toward a tumor. Heavy shielding blocks much of the radiation in other directions. A hospital eventually replaces a source when it gets too weak for treatment. It can still give someone nearby a dangerous dose.
This one was supposed to go back to its supplier in the United States. The company got permission to export it in 1994. Instead, it kept the packages in Ankara. Two eventually went to a warehouse in Istanbul, then into the adjoining building. For months, they sat near the front, behind unlocked doors. When that building was sold, the packages went with it.

## Chapter Three · The Pile
Now the team needs to find the capsule in a yard full of broken metal.
They spot a cylindrical part from one of the containers. It looks like the piece that could still be holding the source. The crew makes a shielded box with lead bricks, then practices lifting a piece of pipe with the scrapyard's mechanical grab.
The real lift works on the first try. The crew drops the cylinder into the box. The readings in the yard stay high. The cylinder they've just recovered is empty.
The detector leads them farther into the yard, to a heap of scrap. The grab lifts pieces away. The radiation readings go up. The pile itself has been shielding the capsule. The more metal they remove, the less protection they have.
They call off the search for the day.
On January tenth, a truck backs into the yard with a radiation detector on a long boom. A thick wall of concrete blocks shields the operator. A crane holds a lead-lined drum. Ten pairs of responders take turns. One person moves pieces of scrap with a long pole. The other shovels anything suspicious into the drum. The crane lifts it so the team can check what's inside.
Ten attempts. Still nothing.
The team considers burying the pile in concrete. That would shield whatever is in there, and they'd lose the chance to identify it. They check the radiation doses recorded by the workers and decide they can make another round of attempts.
On the fourteenth try, they recover the capsule. Its casing is intact. The cobalt has stayed sealed inside all this time. Gamma rays were able to reach people as soon as the shielding was removed.

## Chapter Four · The Second Container
Eighteen people are admitted for observation and treatment. Ten adults are diagnosed with acute radiation sickness. The radiation has damaged the marrow that makes their blood cells. Doctors give some of them platelet transfusions to reduce the risk of bleeding. All ten survive.
By then, investigators have another question. The family bought two containers. Could both have held a source?
An international team retraces the route. They survey the yard beside the house, the second scrapyard and the smelting works, about ninety kilometers east of Istanbul. They find only normal background radiation. Then they compare serial numbers. On which sources were sent back to America and which stayed in Turkey, the company's records and the supplier's records contradict each other.
The investigators conclude that the second container was probably empty. They can't establish that for certain. After the accident, Turkey inspects all forty-six of its radiotherapy centers and tightens the rules for storing and returning used sources.
By the time the man who reached into the container leaves the hospital, the wounds on his fingers have healed. In November, a scan shows that blood is no longer reaching the tip of his index finger.
Surgeons have to remove it.
