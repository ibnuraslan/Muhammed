“I was unfaithful to my wife.” That’s what Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang tells his employees on November eighth. The same day, Nvidia opens an independent investigation into whether a foreign intelligence service targeted him.
The woman at the center of the allegation is Alice Huang. She comes from the other side of Nvidia’s business. The company she helps build buys the kinds of chips Washington is trying to keep out of China.
## Chapter One · Follow the Chips
In October 2022, the United States announces a new set of export controls. Shipments of certain advanced processors to China are restricted. Washington says the same computing power that drives commercial research can also help China build more advanced military systems.
One of the chips caught up in those restrictions is Nvidia’s H100. Put enough of them together in a data center and they can split the work of training an AI model across a whole bank of machines.
For a company trying to get that computing power, there are two ways to go about it. You can buy the hardware and install it yourself. Or you can pay to use someone else’s. The machines can be in one country and the person sending them work can be in another. The data moves. The chips stay in the rack. That distinction matters because a shipping record can tell you where a machine was delivered. A different set of records would tell you who rented it and where they connected from.
That’s the business Alice Huang moves into. She comes from Shanghai’s investment world. In 2023, she takes control of a Singapore company called Megaspeed. It began as a subsidiary of a video game developer. It becomes an Nvidia cloud partner. By the end of 2023, a Singapore company called SwiftData has taken ownership of Megaspeed.
Look at Megaspeed’s filings. In 2023, the company reports thirty-three million dollars in assets. A year later, it reports three billion. And when Alice and a business partner pitch a new data center project to investors in early 2025, an investor tells the Financial Times that the pair makes a point of how easily they can get Nvidia GPUs. The investor says they name Alibaba as a client for the project.
The chip deliveries leave their own trail. Shipping records reviewed by Reuters show Megaspeed’s Indonesian subsidiary importing Nvidia hardware between 2024 and 2026. The records include H100s. Those shipments establish that the hardware reached Indonesia. The next question is who could use it, and from where.
By October 2025, the New York Times reports that U.S. authorities are looking into whether Megaspeed helped Chinese companies get access to advanced chips covered by American export controls. Singapore police confirm their own investigation into possible breaches of Singapore law. Megaspeed denies illicit activity involving chips.
On October tenth, reporters from the Straits Times visit Megaspeed’s office on the seventh floor of Singapore’s Ocean Financial Centre. The glass door is locked. They can see two gray armchairs in reception. The company’s nameplate is still there.
## Chapter Two · The Memo
At the 2024 Computex trade show in Taiwan, a server manufacturer called Gigabyte throws a party. Its servers use Nvidia chips. A person who attends tells Reuters that both Alice and Jensen are there. In a photo posted on LinkedIn, the two are sitting side by side.
Then there’s video. On May twenty-second, 2025, Taiwanese television footage shows a group dinner at a restaurant in Taipei. Reuters later compares the footage with a separate photograph and identifies Alice at the table. Jensen is there too.
Their companies have a clear business connection. The espionage allegation traces back to a document circulating in Washington.
In the spring of 2026, according to the New York Times, a memo circulates among senior officials in the Trump administration. People briefed on the intelligence tell the paper that China’s Ministry of State Security recruited Alice Huang to gather information on Jensen. The reported goal includes learning more about America’s advanced AI chips and potentially getting Nvidia to push for broader sales to China.
The allegation puts a different kind of information at stake. A chip is something you can trace through a shipment. A company’s plans for what it wants to sell, or what it’s asking Washington to approve, can travel in a conversation.
Consider how much a single decision in Washington can change. In April 2025, the U.S. government tells Nvidia it needs a license to sell a different chip, the H20, into China. Nvidia later reports a four and a half billion dollar charge tied to inventory and purchase commitments for that chip. Knowing which processors Washington might clear for sale, and when, could shape where a customer spends billions.
On October twenty-ninth, the Times makes the suspected operation public. Nvidia says its CEO routinely meets with customers and that its regional cloud customers receive ordinary business information. China’s Foreign Ministry later rejects the spying allegation. Reuters says it was unable to verify it independently, and reporters at both Reuters and the Financial Times were unable to reach Alice for comment.
## Chapter Three · Behind Closed Doors
In Washington, three House committee chairs send Jensen a letter. They want him to brief them behind closed doors about his contacts with Alice Huang and what Nvidia does to protect itself from foreign espionage. They request November eighteenth.
Four days after the letter is dated, Jensen sends the email admitting to an affair. He says he’ll cooperate fully with the board’s investigation. His message leaves the other person unnamed. The Times and the Financial Times identify her as Alice Huang.
When Nvidia’s board announces its investigation, it says it has no reason to believe company secrets or national security were compromised. The next day, President Trump says the FBI is looking into the matter. On November tenth, Nvidia names the lawyer who will lead the outside investigation. Joon H. Kim is a former acting U.S. attorney in Manhattan.
A shareholder lawsuit adds another question. The complaint accuses Nvidia and its CEO of misleading investors about the risks surrounding the company’s dealings with Megaspeed. Those allegations are now before a court.
As of November eleventh, there’s been no public finding that Megaspeed broke export rules. Nvidia’s board has yet to publish its conclusions. And the evidence behind the reported intelligence assessment is still out of public view.
If Jensen gives Congress the briefing it requested, it will take place behind closed doors. The question on the other side of those doors is the one the public still can’t answer. What, if anything, reached Beijing?
