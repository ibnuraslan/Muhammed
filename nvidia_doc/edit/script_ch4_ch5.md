# Nvidia — added chapters (they follow "What, if anything, reached Beijing?")

## Chapter Four · The Second Employee

Before that briefing can happen, a reporter puts a different question to Jensen Huang. It’s about a woman inside Nvidia.

Her name is Kimberly Powell. She joined the company in 2008, when her job was to get Nvidia chips into medical imaging equipment. By 2026, she runs the company’s healthcare business, overseeing technology used in everything from medical scans to drug discovery. She reports directly to Huang.

On November eleventh, The New York Times reports that Huang and Powell had an affair, citing five people who worked at Nvidia. When a reporter calls Huang to ask about Powell, he says, “I don’t think you should be talking to me directly.” Then he hangs up, according to the Times.

Powell and Nvidia don’t immediately respond to requests for comment. Reuters says it couldn’t immediately verify the Times report.

Open Nvidia’s code of conduct. The first letter is signed by Jensen Huang. A few pages later, the company lists personal relationships among the things that can create a conflict of interest. If a conflict arises, the code says employees should remove themselves from decisions on the matter. The Times report leaves a question for the company. Did the alleged relationship affect any decisions inside Nvidia?

That night, according to Reuters, Nvidia’s directors meet to consider removing Huang. Three days earlier, the board had publicly expressed confidence in him and his leadership team. Now the company he co-founded is preparing for a change at the top.

The date is in Nvidia’s securities filing. On November eleventh, the board accepts Huang’s resignation, effective immediately. On November twelfth, the company announces it. After thirty-three years, Jensen Huang is out as chief executive. He also resigns as president and gives up his seat on the board.

Colette Kress, Nvidia’s finance chief since 2013, takes over as interim CEO. The directors begin looking for a permanent replacement, and they name longtime director Harvey Jones the company’s first board chair. Nvidia is five days away from reporting its quarterly results.

Then, with the resignation already public, Reuters breaks another story.

Two people familiar with the matter tell Reuters that Huang had an intimate relationship with another Nvidia employee. A different woman from Kimberly Powell. Reuters withholds her name and says it couldn’t reach her. Nvidia declines to comment. Huang, Powell, and a representative for the board don’t respond to Reuters’ requests for comment.

In four days, Nvidia has gone from its founder’s email to a new chief executive and two reports involving women inside the company.

Then a government statement from Singapore puts the focus back on Alice Huang. By the time the public saw the intelligence allegations, Singapore’s police had already taken her passport, given it back, and barred her from returning.

## Chapter Five · The Passport and the Paper Trail

On June twenty-fourth, 2025, Alice Huang is present when officials from the U.S. Commerce Department carry out a post-shipment check at Megaspeed. According to Singapore’s Home Affairs Ministry, that’s when Singapore first learns that American authorities are interested in her and the company.

Singapore’s police and customs officers have already been investigating possible false declarations involving server purchases for four months. That same day, police question Alice. They take her passport so they can secure her attendance during the investigation.

The servers in Singapore’s account have passed through the country on their way to Malaysia. Investigators are trying to find out whether they went farther than the paperwork said they would. To do that, they need records from overseas. One company they turn to is Supermicro, an American server supplier. Singapore says the company provided limited cooperation, and that repeated requests to U.S. authorities brought limited help in obtaining information about it.

By February 2026, Singapore police and prosecutors say they have insufficient evidence to establish that Alice committed an offense tied to the suspected false declarations. Police also say they assessed that she knowingly gave them inaccurate information to downplay her role at Megaspeed. They issue a stern warning and bar her from re-entering Singapore. On February twenty-sixth, she gets her passport back. She leaves the country that day.

Nine months later, Bloomberg reports that Alice is living in Beijing. When asked to confirm that, China’s Foreign Ministry spokesperson says she’s unfamiliar with it. Beijing has denied the spying allegation.

There’s another development in China, and it starts with a shipment that’s waiting to move.

In early November, Reuters reports that about six hundred thousand Nvidia H200 chips are sitting in Hong Kong. Chinese companies want them. Some H200s have already been allowed into mainland China. Beijing is holding back approval for imports on that scale. Reuters had also reported a separate Chinese directive requiring new state-funded data center projects to use domestically made AI chips.

A sale can clear Washington and still get held up on the Chinese side. And for a company that needs computing power now, there’s still the option of renting it overseas. Reuters reports that some Chinese AI companies are trying to secure more of that capacity, worried that Washington could tighten the rules on remote access too.

Against that backdrop, The Information makes a much larger claim about chips physically entering China. It reports that when orders stalled, Alice used her connections to Nvidia and its distributors to secure supply. Its sources say she helped move roughly two hundred and forty thousand chips into China, worth at least seven and a half billion dollars. Reuters says it couldn’t independently verify the account. Megaspeed denies illegally diverting chips and says it has hired a U.S. law firm to inventory its GPUs and review its export compliance.

On November thirteenth, Malaysia confirms that Megaspeed’s local subsidiary, Speedmatrix, imported servers and AI chips as a data center tenant. Its trade ministry says it’s working with customs to establish whether any of those chips were exported again, and whether any such shipments complied with Malaysian law. That gives investigators a concrete place to look. They know which company brought the equipment in. They’re checking what happened after it arrived.

In Washington, Attorney General Todd Blanche confirms the FBI is investigating. He says the Justice Department has yet to reach a conclusion on whether Alice worked for Chinese intelligence.

And the congressional briefing mentioned earlier has moved. The House chairs now want a closed-door, transcribed interview with Jensen Huang on December first. They want Nvidia’s top lawyer the following day. If Huang doesn’t accept, they say they’ll move to subpoena him.

Nvidia can point to a filing and show exactly when its founder left. To answer what reached Beijing, investigators are still looking for the equivalent. A shipping record that shows where the hardware ended up. A customer record that shows who used it. And evidence of what, if anything, passed between Alice Huang and Chinese intelligence.
