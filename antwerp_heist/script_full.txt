Monday morning. A man walks down to the vault beneath Antwerp's Diamond Center.
The lights are on. The door is open. Safe-deposit boxes line the walls, more than a hundred of them broken into. Jewelry, documents, and empty containers cover the floor.
An investigator checks with the alarm monitoring center. The system still reports that the vault is secure.
The thieves have already gone.
Two years earlier, one of them walked into this building and asked to rent an office.
His name is Leonardo Notarbartolo. He comes from Turin, Italy, and works in jewelry. He also has a criminal record that the people renting him the office never check.
In late 2000, he becomes a tenant at the Diamond Center. With the office comes access to the building. He rents a safe-deposit box, too.
Now he has a reason to visit the room he plans to rob.
The vault sits two floors underground. During business hours, its heavy steel door stays open. A locked grille controls access. Clients present themselves to a camera so staff upstairs can recognize them and let them through.
Leonardo can go down, open his box, and leave. Each visit gives him another look at the locks, cameras, and sensors around him.
At night, the steel door closes. Opening it requires a key and a combination. Sensors monitor the door, the light inside, and anyone moving through the vault. Drilling or tunneling risks setting off another alarm.
The alarms are silent. Make a mistake and the first warning could be police entering behind you.
Leonardo studies the people operating those systems.
He learns that the two parts of the vault key, designed to be stored separately, are kept together near the vault. He learns that the security recordings stay in the building. And he gets a copy of its blueprints after expressing interest in renting more office space.
The people helping him have different skills. Elio D'Onorio knows alarm systems. Ferdinando Finotto is another experienced thief. They belong to a loose circle of Italian criminals who share expertise and work together on jobs.
They have time to study this one. Leonardo keeps his office for more than two years.
By February 2003, the team has chosen a weekend. The traders will leave on Friday. They're due back on Monday.
That gives the thieves hours inside, provided they can get through the building without anyone raising an alarm.
On the night of February fifteenth, the crew enters the Diamond Center. Investigators later trace their likely route through the underground garage. Its entrance opens outside the streets most tightly controlled by the diamond district's police.
They make their way down toward the vault.
The surveillance cameras can record them, but the recordings are stored upstairs. The men will take those before they leave.
First, they have to open the steel door.
They recover the key kept nearby. They also get past the combination lock. Exactly how remains one of the gaps in the case. Leonardo later claims a hidden camera captured the combination. Investigators have never established a complete account of how they obtained it.
What they can see afterward is how the crew dealt with the door alarm.
Normally, opening the door separates two magnetic contacts and sends a signal. The thieves keep those contacts together while moving them out of the way. The door can swing open while the alarm still registers it as closed.
Inside, the crew blinds sensors and covers the light detector with tape.
Then they turn on the lights.
They've reached the boxes. Each one has its own lock. The crew uses a specially made tool to force them open.
Diamonds. Gold. Cash. Jewelry.
They pull out the contents and sort through them. Anything they leave behind will help explain what happened, but the bags they're carrying have a limit. Even valuables end up on the floor.
By the time they finish, 109 boxes have been forced open.
The crew takes the security recordings and gets out. No armed response has stopped them. Nobody discovers the break-in until Monday.
When the traders reach the vault, people start searching the wreckage for their own boxes. Some are crying. Some faint.
The estimated loss reaches a hundred million dollars.
Detectives Agim De Bruycker and Patrick Peys now have a room full of evidence. But they're missing the camera recordings that could show who entered it.
Then a call comes in about rubbish dumped in woodland beside a highway.
The man who finds it, August Van Camp, regularly checks the area for illegal dumping. He goes through rubbish looking for something that might identify whoever left it there.
This time, he finds materials connected to the diamond trade.
Police examine the bags. There are documents, receipts, and traces of gems. Among the household waste is a half-eaten salami sandwich.
The crew has thrown away rubbish from the robbery along with things that can lead back to their ordinary lives.
A torn document refers to Leonardo Notarbartolo. Another piece identifies Elio D'Onorio. The name Leonardo leads detectives back to the Diamond Center itself.
He's one of its tenants.
They check with police in Turin. The jeweler who rented an office above the vault is already known to Italian investigators.
Other scraps lead farther. A supermarket receipt gives police a purchase to check against the shop's security footage. They identify Finotto.
The sandwich goes for testing. It will provide DNA evidence linking Leonardo to the discarded rubbish.
Five days after the robbery, Leonardo returns to the Diamond Center.
He uses his access badge. He's still a tenant, still someone with a reason to be here. But staff have already been answering questions about him.
The building manager calls police, then keeps him talking until they arrive.
When investigators search Leonardo's apartment, they find more gems in the carpet. Evidence from the vault, the rubbish, and the apartment links the men together.
In 2005, Leonardo is sentenced to ten years for organizing the robbery. Three accomplices receive five-year sentences.
Years later, he offers a different explanation. A diamond dealer hired the crew, he says. The robbery was part of an insurance scam. He describes a replica vault built for rehearsals and claims the haul was smaller than everyone believes.
Investigators challenge that account. They find no evidence of the supposed organizer. And the idea that the men only discovered what was in the bags after escaping conflicts with the scene inside the vault. The lights were on. The contents had already been sorted.
The men are convicted. Most of the loot remains missing.
Back in the vault, the tape over the light detector explains why the thieves could see. The displaced contacts explain why the open door didn't bring police. The office lease explains how Leonardo got close enough to study it all.
But the first document to put his name in the detectives' hands is found miles away, in the rubbish his own crew left beside the road.
