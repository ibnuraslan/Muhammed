Jean-Claude Romand stops his car in a forest outside Paris and tells the woman with him that he has a present for her. They are supposed to be going to dinner at the country home of an important politician. Now he says he wants to give her a necklace.
But the object he has brought is a nylon cord. The woman turns, and the encounter becomes an attack. Romand sprays her with tear gas. She pleads with him to stop.
Her name is Chantal. She has trusted this man with a large amount of money. She also believes he is a doctor.
Before this night is over, he will drive her back to her apartment and ask her to keep quiet. What she does not yet know is that his wife, his two children, and both his parents are already dead.
To the people around him, Romand has been a successful medical researcher, a husband, a father, and a dependable member of the family. The investigation will discover that the researcher has no job, the doctor has no qualification, and the money supporting his household belongs to people who think he is investing it for them.
The murders happen in January 1993. The deception that precedes them takes us back eighteen years, to medical school in Lyon.
In 1975, Romand has not passed the examination that allows him to advance beyond his second year. But he tells the people around him that he has succeeded. It is a lie he can still correct. He can admit the failure, repeat the year openly, or leave medicine. Instead, he keeps the successful version of himself in circulation.
At university, he repeatedly registers for the same year while maintaining the appearance of progressing through his studies. He attends courses and reads medical material. There are books and notes for other people to see. As the years pass, his friends are completing real stages of their training, while he describes stages of a career he is inventing.
One of those friends later remembers seeing him surrounded by the equipment of a serious student: folders, journals, lecture notes. He has no reason to treat a friend’s account of passing an examination as a claim that needs investigating. Romand has attended medical school with him. He knows the subject. The story fits what is already visible.
Romand is acquiring medical knowledge. What he lacks is the qualification and professional life he claims it represents.
In 1980, he marries Florence Crolet. They have known each other through their families since they were young. She trains as a pharmacist. Their circle includes medical professionals, people who might seem well placed to recognize an impostor. Yet Romand can discuss medical research convincingly enough to belong among them.
Over time, he gives himself a position at the World Health Organization in Geneva. He presents himself as a researcher, which also provides an explanation for why he does not have an ordinary medical practice. His work takes place elsewhere, inside a large international institution.
The family eventually lives in Prévessin-Moëns, on the French side of the Swiss border. Geneva is close enough for the journey to work to be entirely plausible. Romand leaves home for a working day. But there is no office expecting him, no research team waiting for his contribution, and no salary arriving from the organization.
He spends time in libraries, cafés, and his car. He reads, waits, and returns home. He can also enter publicly accessible areas of the WHO and collect its publications. Genuine medical information helps him maintain an invented professional identity.
A visitor can leave with genuine publications, read about real research, and return home able to discuss it. To someone watching from outside, that can look very much like a working day.
His claimed professional trips require another arrangement. Accounts of his deception describe him staying near Geneva airport while his family believes he is abroad. He reads about the places he is supposedly visiting and brings back souvenirs. A trip can therefore produce the expected objects and stories without the journey ever taking place.
Contact with him is controlled as well. His family does not simply call through to a desk at the organization. Messages go through an answering service, and he calls back. His professional and private worlds remain separated. The people who know him at home have little opportunity to compare their version of his life with a colleague’s.
But an imaginary job creates a problem that medical reading cannot solve. The family needs money.
There is help from his parents and money from property. Eventually, Romand offers relatives something more attractive: access to investments in Switzerland. His supposed international position, he explains, gives him opportunities other people cannot obtain so easily.
Florence’s brother, Emmanuel Crolet, later describes how quietly the idea spreads. Romand does not have to pressure someone across a table. He lets people know that he can help. When a relative has money available, another member of the family may remember the offer.
That makes the arrangement feel like a benefit of knowing him. The man proposing the investment is already part of their lives. They have attended family gatherings with him. They know his parents. They have watched him marry Florence and raise children. The apparent success of his household supplies reassurance.
Emmanuel remembers promised returns of around sixteen percent. Money passes to Romand on the understanding that it will be placed in Switzerland. Instead, he uses entrusted funds to support his own life.
The deception now begins to reinforce itself. People believe he has money because he has a successful career. Their belief persuades them to give him money. Spending it helps him continue to look like a successful professional.
But their savings remain a debt. Whatever he spends on ordinary life is money he may one day be asked to return. His supposed investments cannot produce the missing funds, because he has not made them.
The financial exploitation extends beyond investment offers. Emmanuel also describes a relative with cancer being offered access to supposed special medicines through Romand’s research connections. The prestigious job becomes a way to sell hope to someone who is ill.
By this point, Romand is maintaining more than an impressive biography. Other people are making financial decisions around it. His claims can affect how they plan for retirement, how they use their savings, and what they believe might help a sick relative.
In 1988, his father-in-law, Pierre Crolet, asks for the return of some money he has entrusted to him. Later that year, Pierre suffers a fatal fall at home while Romand is present.
The circumstances subsequently attract suspicion. Romand denies responsibility, and he is not prosecuted over the death. It cannot be presented as an established additional murder. What is established is the financial problem behind the request: money that relatives believe is available has been disappearing into Romand’s life.
Meanwhile, he begins a relationship with Chantal. She also entrusts him with money—nine hundred thousand francs. Eventually, she wants it returned.
A reassuring explanation can delay that conversation. It cannot repay her.
By the end of 1992, the account of a wealthy researcher is increasingly difficult to maintain. Early investigators will find that his bank accounts have gone into overdraft. The man who supposedly knows how to make other people’s money grow is running out of money himself.
There is pressure inside the family too. Emmanuel remembers a changed atmosphere at Christmas. He later comes to believe that Florence has begun discovering what her husband is hiding. That is his interpretation; precisely what Florence knew, and what passed between them before her death, will remain unresolved.
The reconstruction of what happens inside the house will depend partly on Romand’s own account. The people who could contradict him will not survive.
On January ninth, 1993, he kills Florence in their home with a rolling pin. She is thirty-seven. Their daughter Caroline is seven, and their son Antoine is five.
In his later account to the court, the children come downstairs and watch a cartoon. Their father is still moving around the house with them. He then takes them upstairs separately and shoots them with a rifle.
He describes telling Caroline that she has a fever before having her lie down. A claim about her health becomes a way to secure her cooperation. In the account he gives, the familiar authority of a parent is still operating, even as he uses it to kill.
Afterward, he leaves the house and travels to his parents in Clairvaux-les-Lacs. Aimé and Anne-Marie receive their son. He eats lunch with them. Then he retrieves the rifle and kills them too.
The sequence includes journeys, a meal, and actions carried out between the killings. It gives investigators much more to examine than a single brief eruption of violence. Romand will later struggle to explain parts of it, particularly the killing of Florence. But his uncertainty about his motives does not erase the acts themselves.
By the afternoon, five people are dead. He still has an arrangement with Chantal in Paris.
He travels to meet her and offers another account of his powerful connections. They are going to dinner with Bernard Kouchner, a prominent French political and humanitarian figure. The proposed destination is a country house near Fontainebleau.
Romand does not have the friendship he claims. But the invitation is consistent with the man Chantal believes she knows. Important people and privileged access have been part of his story for years.
They drive out of Paris. The journey takes them into the forest. He pretends to have lost his way, stops, and goes to the boot of the car.
This is the encounter from the beginning of the story: the supposed present, the cord, and the tear gas.
Chantal survives. Romand stops the attack and supplies another explanation for his behavior. He claims to be seriously ill. He takes her back and urges her not to tell anyone. There is even a further arrangement concerning the repayment of her money.
After killing five members of his family and attacking a sixth person, he is still making promises he cannot fulfill.
He returns to the house in Prévessin-Moëns, where Florence and the children remain. During the night leading into Monday, January eleventh, he spreads petrol and sets the house on fire. He also takes barbiturates.
Firefighters find him unconscious but alive. He is taken to hospital in Geneva.
Florence and the children cannot be saved. Examination establishes that they were killed before the fire. His parents are also discovered shot dead in their home. What could initially look like a domestic fire now connects two separate crime scenes and five deliberate killings.
Romand survives, and the investigation begins dismantling the professional identity his family has accepted.
The World Health Organization has never employed him. His supposed medical qualification does not exist. University records show the repeated registrations that belong to a different history from the one he has been telling.
Friends are confronted with two discoveries at once. Someone they trusted has killed his family. The person they thought they knew has also been misrepresenting his working life for years.
A doctor who knew him from university explains how convincing his medical knowledge had been. The conversations were convincing. His university records tell a different story.
The finances make the deception more concrete. Money has reached Romand from people who expected him to invest it. Investigators can follow payments and balances. The comfortable household has been supported by funds entrusted to him under false pretenses.
Romand is charged while still in hospital. He eventually faces trial before the assize court in Bourg-en-Bresse in June 1996.
The court must determine his criminal responsibility, but the killings themselves are not the central factual dispute. The deeper struggle is to understand the relationship between the years of deception and the violence that followed.
When asked about the children and his parents, Romand breaks down. Proceedings are interrupted. At other moments he speaks quietly. People who have spent years trying to understand him must now decide what weight to give the explanations of a man whose previous explanations sustained the entire fraud.
He describes fear of disappointing the people close to him. He presents the deaths in terms of the suffering they would experience after the truth emerged.
That is his account. It does not establish that his victims faced an intolerable future, and it gives him no authority to decide whether they should live through it.
The court also examines his apparent attempt to die in the fire. He insists that he intended to kill himself. The prosecution questions his actions and the circumstances of his survival. His intentions remain disputed, while the outcome is clear: the people he killed are dead, and he has been rescued.
Psychiatric experts examine him, but the trial does not produce a complete, agreed explanation for the massacre. A description of his personality cannot reconstruct every thought or conversation inside the house.
For Florence’s family, some questions are permanently beyond reach. Her brother can describe what he observed at Christmas. Romand can offer his version of their final days. Florence cannot give hers.
On July second, 1996, Romand is sentenced to life imprisonment, with a twenty-two-year period during which he cannot be considered for release. He is convicted of the five murders, the attempted murder of Chantal, and the misappropriation of two and a half million francs between 1987 and 1993.
In prison, another phase of his life begins. He works as a librarian and restores audiovisual archives. His case becomes the subject of Emmanuel Carrère’s book The Adversary and later adaptations.
By 2019, the question before the courts has changed. After decades in custody, can he be released under conditions?
An initial application is rejected. An appeal succeeds. In June 2019, after twenty-six years of detention, he leaves prison and moves into a Benedictine abbey at Fontgombault.
His release includes electronic monitoring and restrictions. A later report by France Télévisions confirms that his electronic bracelet was removed in 2021.
Emmanuel Crolet publicly objects to the idea that the passage of time has brought resolution. He has lost his sister, his niece, and his nephew. The end of Romand’s imprisonment does not end those losses.
“His time in prison is over,” Emmanuel says. “Our pain will last forever.”
