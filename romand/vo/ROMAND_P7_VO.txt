The World Health Organization has never employed him. His supposed medical qualification does not exist. University records show the repeated registrations that belong to a different history from the one he has been telling.

Friends are confronted with two discoveries at once. Someone they trusted has killed his family. The person they thought they knew has also been misrepresenting his working life for years.

A doctor who knew him from university explains how convincing his medical knowledge had been. The conversations were convincing. His university records tell a different story.

The finances make the deception more concrete. Money has reached Romand from people who expected him to invest it. Investigators can follow payments and balances. The comfortable household has been supported by funds entrusted to him under false pretenses.

Romand is charged while still in hospital. He eventually faces trial before the assize court in Bourg-en-Bresse in June 1996.

The court must determine his criminal responsibility, but the killings themselves are not the central factual dispute. The deeper struggle is to understand the relationship between the years of deception and the violence that followed.

When asked about the children and his parents, Romand breaks down. Proceedings are interrupted. At other moments he speaks quietly. People who have spent years trying to understand him must now decide what weight to give the explanations of a man whose previous explanations sustained the entire fraud.

He describes fear of disappointing the people close to him. He presents the deaths in terms of the suffering they would experience after the truth emerged.

That is his account. It does not establish that his victims faced an intolerable future, and it gives him no authority to decide whether they should live through it.
