By the end of 1992, the account of a wealthy researcher is increasingly difficult to maintain. Early investigators will find that his bank accounts have gone into overdraft. The man who supposedly knows how to make other people’s money grow is running out of money himself.

There is pressure inside the family too. Emmanuel remembers a changed atmosphere at Christmas. He later comes to believe that Florence has begun discovering what her husband is hiding. That is his interpretation; precisely what Florence knew, and what passed between them before her death, will remain unresolved.

The reconstruction of what happens inside the house will depend partly on Romand’s own account. The people who could contradict him will not survive.

On January ninth, 1993, he kills Florence in their home with a rolling pin. She is thirty-seven. Their daughter Caroline is seven, and their son Antoine is five.

In his later account to the court, the children come downstairs and watch a cartoon. Their father is still moving around the house with them. He then takes them upstairs separately and shoots them with a rifle.

He describes telling Caroline that she has a fever before having her lie down. A claim about her health becomes a way to secure her cooperation. In the account he gives, the familiar authority of a parent is still operating, even as he uses it to kill.

Afterward, he leaves the house and travels to his parents in Clairvaux-les-Lacs. Aimé and Anne-Marie receive their son. He eats lunch with them. Then he retrieves the rifle and kills them too.

The sequence includes journeys, a meal, and actions carried out between the killings. It gives investigators much more to examine than a single brief eruption of violence. Romand will later struggle to explain parts of it, particularly the killing of Florence. But his uncertainty about his motives does not erase the acts themselves.

By the afternoon, five people are dead. He still has an arrangement with Chantal in Paris.
