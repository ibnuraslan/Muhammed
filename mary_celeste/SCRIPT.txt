Oliver Deveau steps onto a ship that's still moving. He searches the deck, then goes below. In the captain's cabin, the bedding is wet. On the bed is an impression that looks to him as though a child has been lying there.
He finds no child. He finds no one.
Back on deck, a section of railing has been taken out and laid flat. There's a gap wide enough to launch a small boat. The ship's boat is gone.
Deveau goes back for two men. Then the three of them climb aboard and sail the ship to Gibraltar.
Ten people had been on the Mary Celeste. Whatever made them leave, the ship survived it.
On December fourth, 1872, Deveau is first mate aboard the Dei Gratia, a cargo ship crossing the Atlantic. Shortly after noon, the man at the wheel spots sails in the distance. Captain David Morehouse calls Deveau up from below. They change course to see if the other ship needs help.
As they come within hailing distance, the crew calls out. Nobody answers. A few sails are still drawing wind. Others are torn away or hanging loose. Morehouse sends men across to board her.
On the deck of the Mary Celeste, Deveau finds the compass at the helm smashed. Two hatch covers are off. Down in the galley, water has sloshed high enough to reach the raised lip around the doorway. The stove has been knocked out of place.
He measures the water in the bottom of the ship. Three and a half feet.
The hull looks in good shape. The masts are standing. There's enough sail left to keep her moving. The question is whether three men can handle her and keep the water down all the way to Gibraltar.
Before he takes that chance, Deveau finds something that tells him where to start looking for the people who were here. In the mate's cabin is the ship's logbook. On the cabin table is the slate the crew used to jot things down during a watch.
The book stops on November twenty-fourth. The slate carries on into the next morning. It puts the Mary Celeste six miles from Santa Maria, an island in the Azores.
Deveau is hundreds of miles east of that island. He has no record of what happened in between.
Less than a month earlier, the Mary Celeste is still in New York. Her captain, Benjamin Briggs, writes home as the ship gets ready to leave. He likes the way she sits in the water. He's never sailed her before, so he can't yet say how she handles.
His wife Sarah has joined him with Sophia, their two-year-old daughter. Their son Arthur is seven. He stays in Massachusetts to go to school. Briggs tells his mother that they already miss him. He jokes that he would have sent for the boy if he could have found somewhere to put him.
Space is tight below. One of Sarah's visitors bumps her bonnet coming down into the cabin. At the same time, men are loading seventeen hundred and one barrels of commercial alcohol for Genoa. Sarah writes about the racket as the cargo comes aboard. There's enough banging to make it hard to think.
The ship leaves the pier on November fifth, then anchors off Staten Island. The wind is against them, and Briggs sees little point in fighting it. Two days later, Sarah writes again. The wind has turned in their favor. The pilot takes her letter ashore.
That's the last letter anyone receives from the ship.
On November twenty-fifth, Santa Maria comes into view. The crew can see land after crossing most of the Atlantic. Somewhere on the other side of that entry on the slate, the record goes blank. Nobody writes down a reason to stop. Nobody records putting the boat in the water.
When Deveau boards days later, some of the things a sailor would want in a small boat are gone. The ship's chronometer, a precise clock used to work out longitude, is missing. So is the sextant, which helps fix a position from the sun or stars. Some of the ship's papers are gone, too. The captain's clothes and other belongings are still below.
Look at what's missing together. A small boat. Tools for navigation. Papers. Then look at the gap in the rail.
That's consistent with people launching a boat and taking things they might need once they were in it. It still leaves Deveau with three and a half feet of water to deal with and a ship he believes he can save.
Morehouse warns him that taking her in could put both crews at risk. The Dei Gratia will have to give up men it needs for its own voyage. Deveau goes anyway. He brings two sailors, his own navigation instruments, and food from his ship. They pump out the Mary Celeste and put up a spare sail in place of one she's lost.
For the first few days, they make progress. Then a gale catches them near the Strait of Gibraltar. Deveau can't make the bay. He shelters off the North African coast, then off Spain. By the time he finally brings the ship in on December thirteenth, the Dei Gratia is already there.
The next day, he writes to his wife about how exhausted he is. He says he wouldn't take on the job again. He also asks whether his little boy, James, can talk yet.
At Gibraltar, the authorities seize the Mary Celeste while a court decides what to pay the men who saved her. Deveau and the crew stand to earn a share of the value they brought into port. That gives the court a reason to go through their story in detail. It also gives Gibraltar's attorney general, Frederick Solly Flood, a question to pursue. Could the men who found the ship know more than they're saying?
Flood has the vessel inspected. A diver goes under her and checks the hull for damage. He finds no sign of a collision. The surveyor comes away unable to explain why anybody would have abandoned the vessel.
Flood looks for evidence of violence. There are marks on the deck that he thinks might be blood. There's also a sword from beneath the captain's berth. Deveau says he drew it from its sheath when he first boarded. It looked rusty to him.
The marks and the sword go for chemical examination. The examination finds no trace of blood.
The court hears evidence for months. It awards the salvors seventeen hundred pounds. No murder is established. By then, the owners have recovered the ship and put a new captain and crew on board. The Mary Celeste can finish the voyage Briggs began.
When the cargo is unloaded in Genoa, nine barrels are empty.
For anyone trying to explain why Briggs might have put his family in a small boat, those barrels are worth a closer look. If alcohol leaked into the hold, vapor could have collected below deck. The people above might have feared a fire or an explosion. An incident like that could give a captain a reason to get everyone away from a ship that otherwise looked capable of sailing.
There are problems with that explanation. Deveau's party reports no smell of alcohol fumes. The main cargo hatch is still secured when they board. The nine empty barrels were described as red oak, a more porous wood than the white oak used for the others. That gives the missing alcohol a possible explanation on its own. Nobody can tell from the empty barrels whether they leaked slowly or whether anything happened suddenly enough to make ten people climb over the side.
There's another clue Deveau finds before anyone counts the barrels. One of the ship's pumps has been taken apart.
The ship had previously carried coal and had recently been refitted. More than a century later, documentary researcher Anne MacGregor looks at those two facts alongside the dismantled pump. Coal dust or debris from the work could have fouled it. A pump in pieces suggests someone may have been trying to find out why it wouldn't work. The record can't tell us what they found.
MacGregor and oceanographer Phil Richardson also go back to the slate. They have two points to work with. The last recorded sighting of Santa Maria, and the place Deveau's ship found the Mary Celeste. Using historical weather data, they test whether a ship with nobody steering could get from one to the other. It could.
That matters because it puts a possible evacuation within sight of land. After a stretch of rough weather, Briggs may have been trying to judge how much water was coming in and whether the pumps could keep up. With the hold packed full of barrels, he couldn't simply look down and see what was happening at the bottom. If he believed the water was winning, Santa Maria would have been the one place he could see that offered a way off the ship.
That's one possible reading of the evidence. The water Deveau measures is what's there when he boards. We don't have the reading Briggs was working with, or even know that water was the danger that sent them over the side.
A boat could also have been lowered as a temporary precaution. If the crew meant to stay close and go back aboard once it was safe, they needed some way to stay with the ship. A sailing vessel can move away on the wind. A small boat carrying ten people may have no way to catch it. There's no surviving account that tells us whether they tried to stay alongside, tried to reach the island, or got that far at all.
Years later, stories put a warm meal on the cabin table. That detail makes it sound as if the crew vanished in the middle of dinner. The men who searched the ship describe a soaked cabin and a galley with water in it. The object they found on the table was a slate, and the last position written on it was already days old.
Some of what they found made its way home. The Briggs family's account describes packages arriving with the seal of the American consul in Gibraltar. Inside were Sarah's small reed organ, Benjamin's sword, and two of Sophia's dolls. One had a broken arm.
Arthur was still in Massachusetts. When his parents left, his father wrote that he wished he could find room for him aboard.
There's a record of the ship reaching Gibraltar. There's a record of the cargo reaching Genoa. There's even a record of Sophia's dolls making it back to her brother.
The boat and the ten people who may have been in it were never found.
