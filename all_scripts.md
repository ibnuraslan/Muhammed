# 1. The Spy Allegations Rocking Nvidia

## Intro  ·  72 words, about 35 s

“I was unfaithful to my wife.” That’s what Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang tells his employees on November eighth. The same day, Nvidia opens an independent investigation into whether a foreign intelligence service targeted him.

The woman at the center of the allegation is Alice Huang. She comes from the other side of Nvidia’s business. The company she helps build buys the kinds of chips Washington is trying to keep out of China.

## Chapter One · Follow the Chips  ·  428 words, about 205 s

In October 2022, the United States announces a new set of export controls. Shipments of certain advanced processors to China are restricted. Washington says the same computing power that drives commercial research can also help China build more advanced military systems.

One of the chips caught up in those restrictions is Nvidia’s H100. Put enough of them together in a data center and they can split the work of training an AI model across a whole bank of machines.

For a company trying to get that computing power, there are two ways to go about it. You can buy the hardware and install it yourself. Or you can pay to use someone else’s. The machines can be in one country and the person sending them work can be in another. The data moves. The chips stay in the rack. That distinction matters because a shipping record can tell you where a machine was delivered. A different set of records would tell you who rented it and where they connected from.

That’s the business Alice Huang moves into. She comes from Shanghai’s investment world. In 2023, she takes control of a Singapore company called Megaspeed. It began as a subsidiary of a video game developer. It becomes an Nvidia cloud partner. By the end of 2023, a Singapore company called SwiftData has taken ownership of Megaspeed.

Look at Megaspeed’s filings. In 2023, the company reports thirty-three million dollars in assets. A year later, it reports three billion. And when Alice and a business partner pitch a new data center project to investors in early 2025, an investor tells the Financial Times that the pair makes a point of how easily they can get Nvidia GPUs. The investor says they name Alibaba as a client for the project.

The chip deliveries leave their own trail. Shipping records reviewed by Reuters show Megaspeed’s Indonesian subsidiary importing Nvidia hardware between 2024 and 2026. The records include H100s. Those shipments establish that the hardware reached Indonesia. The next question is who could use it, and from where.

By October 2025, the New York Times reports that U.S. authorities are looking into whether Megaspeed helped Chinese companies get access to advanced chips covered by American export controls. Singapore police confirm their own investigation into possible breaches of Singapore law. Megaspeed denies illicit activity involving chips.

On October tenth, reporters from the Straits Times visit Megaspeed’s office on the seventh floor of Singapore’s Ocean Financial Centre. The glass door is locked. They can see two gray armchairs in reception. The company’s nameplate is still there.

## Chapter Two · The Memo  ·  338 words, about 162 s

At the 2024 Computex trade show in Taiwan, a server manufacturer called Gigabyte throws a party. Its servers use Nvidia chips. A person who attends tells Reuters that both Alice and Jensen are there. In a photo posted on LinkedIn, the two are sitting side by side.

Then there’s video. On May twenty-second, 2025, Taiwanese television footage shows a group dinner at a restaurant in Taipei. Reuters later compares the footage with a separate photograph and identifies Alice at the table. Jensen is there too.

Their companies have a clear business connection. The espionage allegation traces back to a document circulating in Washington.

In the spring of 2026, according to the New York Times, a memo circulates among senior officials in the Trump administration. People briefed on the intelligence tell the paper that China’s Ministry of State Security recruited Alice Huang to gather information on Jensen. The reported goal includes learning more about America’s advanced AI chips and potentially getting Nvidia to push for broader sales to China.

The allegation puts a different kind of information at stake. A chip is something you can trace through a shipment. A company’s plans for what it wants to sell, or what it’s asking Washington to approve, can travel in a conversation.

Consider how much a single decision in Washington can change. In April 2025, the U.S. government tells Nvidia it needs a license to sell a different chip, the H20, into China. Nvidia later reports a four and a half billion dollar charge tied to inventory and purchase commitments for that chip. Knowing which processors Washington might clear for sale, and when, could shape where a customer spends billions.

On October twenty-ninth, the Times makes the suspected operation public. Nvidia says its CEO routinely meets with customers and that its regional cloud customers receive ordinary business information. China’s Foreign Ministry later rejects the spying allegation. Reuters says it was unable to verify it independently, and reporters at both Reuters and the Financial Times were unable to reach Alice for comment.

## Chapter Three · Behind Closed Doors  ·  244 words, about 117 s

In Washington, three House committee chairs send Jensen a letter. They want him to brief them behind closed doors about his contacts with Alice Huang and what Nvidia does to protect itself from foreign espionage. They request November eighteenth.

Four days after the letter is dated, Jensen sends the email admitting to an affair. He says he’ll cooperate fully with the board’s investigation. His message leaves the other person unnamed. The Times and the Financial Times identify her as Alice Huang.

When Nvidia’s board announces its investigation, it says it has no reason to believe company secrets or national security were compromised. The next day, President Trump says the FBI is looking into the matter. On November tenth, Nvidia names the lawyer who will lead the outside investigation. Joon H. Kim is a former acting U.S. attorney in Manhattan.

A shareholder lawsuit adds another question. The complaint accuses Nvidia and its CEO of misleading investors about the risks surrounding the company’s dealings with Megaspeed. Those allegations are now before a court.

As of November eleventh, there’s been no public finding that Megaspeed broke export rules. Nvidia’s board has yet to publish its conclusions. And the evidence behind the reported intelligence assessment is still out of public view.

If Jensen gives Congress the briefing it requested, it will take place behind closed doors. The question on the other side of those doors is the one the public still can’t answer. What, if anything, reached Beijing?

# 2. The Phone That Killed a Banker

## Intro  ·  73 words, about 35 s

Look at the plastic on this telephone. There's a faint stain.

The phone belongs to a Moscow banker. By the time investigators examine it, he and his secretary are both dead.

The substance on the handset is still affecting people who come into the office. Among them are eight police officers.

When investigators search the bank, they find the clue on the one thing sitting in plain sight on his desk. His telephone.

## Chapter One · Two Hospitals  ·  385 words, about 185 s

On August first, 1995, Ivan Kivelidi is at work in Moscow. He runs Rosbusinessbank. That afternoon, he makes several phone calls from the office, according to reporting at the time. His brother comes by and finds him looking unwell. Kivelidi has a history of kidney trouble. There's already a familiar explanation for why he might suddenly need a doctor.

By the time he reaches the hospital, he's in a coma.

The next evening, an ambulance takes another person from the same bank to a different hospital. Her name is Zara Ismailova. She's Kivelidi's secretary. She's unconscious when she arrives. At six the next morning, she dies. Kivelidi dies the following day.

The pathologist assigned to examine Zara is a doctor named Iosif Laskavy. He later remembers opening her file and seeing two possible diagnoses, each followed by a question mark. Epilepsy. A problem with blood flow to the brain. Neither, he says, fits what he sees in the chart. Around the same time, he hears on the radio that Kivelidi has died. He holds off on the autopsy.

Later, forensic experts compare the records from the two hospitals. Both patients had constricted pupils. And in blood samples from both, the activity of an enzyme called cholinesterase had dropped almost to zero.

When a nerve tells a muscle to move, it releases a chemical signal. An enzyme breaks down the chemical so the signal can end. Block the enzyme and the signal keeps acting. Muscles can twitch, then stop working. Eventually, a person can lose the ability to breathe.

Back at the bank, investigators find a faint mark on the handset of a white desk phone. The case files describe residue beneath a small rubber plug in the handset.

It's Kivelidi's phone.

How Zara came into contact with it is less clear. One account says she may have wiped the handset. The exact moment she was exposed remains uncertain. But the phone is still a hazard days later. Among those affected, the files list a cleaner and officers who inspect the room.

At Kivelidi's memorial in Moscow City Hall, the people who come to pay their respects include Mikhail Gorbachev and Russia's prime minister. At the bank, the investigation comes down to a much shorter list. Who had been inside that office before Kivelidi fell ill?

## Chapter Two · The Man in the Office  ·  297 words, about 143 s

One name stands out. Vladimir Khutsishvili is a business partner of Kivelidi's and sits on the bank's board. He visits the office that day. Investigators believe he spends time alone inside. They also find a potential source of friction. Kivelidi plans to issue new shares in the bank, which could reduce Khutsishvili's stake.

Police detain him that autumn. Thirty days later, they let him go. They have a visitor and a possible motive. They still need to show how he could have obtained the poison.

The lab work points investigators toward a class of chemicals that includes some of the most toxic nerve agents ever made. And as the investigation develops, the trail leads to a closed town in Russia's Saratov region.

In Shikhany, Leonid Rink works at a state research institute that develops chemical weapons. Years later, in testimony published by the Russian newspaper Novaya Gazeta, Rink describes a handover near Moscow's Belorussky railway station. A man he'd met through an acquaintance had asked him for poison, supposedly for a dog. Rink says he later supplied a different substance and was paid for it. In court he puts the payment at fifteen hundred dollars. He says the men knew where his daughter and his mother lived and that he gave in after they threatened his family.

Investigators believe that a batch of eight or nine glass ampoules was made for sale. Prosecutors trace an ampoule through intermediaries to Khutsishvili. In their account, he takes it to the bank and puts the poison on the handset while Kivelidi is out of the room.

By December 1999, prosecutors are looking for Khutsishvili again. He's gone abroad. Nearly seven years pass before he returns to Moscow. In June 2006, police arrest him outside an apartment building on Yefremova Street.

## Chapter Three · The Poison Without a Name  ·  291 words, about 140 s

When the case goes to trial, the hearings are closed. Prosecutors argue the evidence contains sensitive information about the poison. Khutsishvili denies killing Kivelidi. His defense says the office door was open and people could see inside. It disputes that he had the opportunity to do what prosecutors describe.

On December twenty-fourth, 2007, the court sentences him to nine years. It finds him guilty of intentionally killing Kivelidi and of causing Zara Ismailova's death through negligence. He later wins early release. The conviction stands.

The court reaches a verdict without publicly naming the compound. Then, in 2018, a poisoning in England brings reporters back to the old case files.

British scientists identify a nerve agent from the Novichok family in the attack on Sergei Skripal and his daughter, Yulia. The British public inquiry later describes how the poison was placed on the handle of Skripal's front door. In Moscow, the object had been an office phone.

Novaya Gazeta publishes material from the Russian investigation, including an analysis of residue from the handset. Vladimir Uglev, who helped develop Soviet nerve agents, tells reporters that one of those agents was used on Kivelidi. Other specialists dispute what can be determined from the published analysis. Rink gives a new account, too. He tells the news outlet The Bell that he handed over rat poison in transactions monitored by the security services. That account conflicts with his earlier testimony.

The evidence photograph is still there. It shows a white handset and a faint mark on the plastic. The court decided who put the poison there. The files that later became public leave two questions open. Exactly what was on that phone? And how many of the other ampoules ever made it back into official hands?

# 3. The Typewriters That Spied on America

## Intro  ·  96 words, about 46 s

Moscow. May, 1984.

A handwritten note is delivered to the American ambassador. The NSA has come to replace the embassy's communications equipment. Most of his staff will hear that they're getting an upgrade.

Over the next several days, workers haul eleven tons of old equipment out of the building. Copiers. Computers. Encryption machines. All of it is going back to the United States.

Washington suspects the Soviets have planted a spy device somewhere in that shipment. The Americans are looking hardest at the machines that send secret messages.

The one they're looking for is a typewriter.

## Chapter One · The Swap  ·  360 words, about 173 s

In August 1983, the French show the Americans a bug they've found in their embassy equipment. It can be controlled from a distance and sends its signal in bursts. An ordinary sweep could miss it. The NSA has to consider that somewhere in the American embassy, there might be a device doing the same thing.

Walter Deeley, the official in charge of communications security at the NSA, helps put together a plan. Take the embassy's communications and information equipment out of Moscow. Get it back to the United States, where technicians can open it up and look properly. Keep the embassy working the entire time.

President Reagan authorizes the operation in February 1984. It's called Project Gunman.

One of the first problems is figuring out what's in the building. There's no central inventory. The NSA learns that the embassy has roughly two hundred and fifty IBM Selectric typewriters. The plant in Lexington, Kentucky, can spare fifty. The team sends those to replace the machines in the most sensitive areas. Its search is still focused on the equipment that handles communications and encryption.

In May, the replacement equipment is staged in Frankfurt and flown to Moscow in batches. The crates are wrapped in burlap to mark them as diplomatic cargo. The staples and seals carry microscopic markings, so inspectors can tell if somebody has replaced them. Light sensors record when the doors of the trailers are opened.

At the embassy, Ambassador Arthur Hartman gets word of the real operation in a handwritten note delivered in person. What most of his staff hear is that the embassy is getting an upgrade.

Then the elevator loses power. The building has a hoist on the roof. Workers use it to bring equipment up the outside, and stage the swap in the attic. An NSA employee runs wires from alarms up there down to the Marines on the sixth floor. Crews carry the new machines downstairs and haul the old ones back up. Some of the teletypes barely fit through the stairways.

About eleven tons go back to the United States. Buried in that shipment is the typewriter that will give them their first lead.

## Chapter Two · The X-Ray  ·  181 words, about 87 s

At Fort Meade, a team of about twenty-five people examines the equipment. They work six days a week. They start with the gear they most expect to find a bug in. For weeks, they come up empty.

Then they turn to the typewriters.

On the evening of July twenty-third, technician Michael Arneson notices an extra coil in the power switch of an IBM Selectric. He X-rays the whole machine. The film shows a cluster of electronics inside a bar that runs the length of the machine.

Arneson is alone. His wife works for the NSA, too. The operation is so tightly held that he can't even tell her what he's found.

The next morning, the engineers debate whether they're looking at a spy device or a feature IBM put there. Some typewriters do have electronic memory. But the circuits on this film are tucked inside what looks like a solid piece of metal.

They take apart two Selectrics side by side, one ordinary, one altered. From the outside, the two bars look alike. Inside one of them is a radio transmitter.

## Chapter Three · The Signal  ·  231 words, about 111 s

Press a key on a Selectric and a little ball covered with raised characters tilts and turns. It brings the right letter up against the ribbon and strikes it onto the paper. Underneath, a set of small metal arms controls that movement.

In the modified machine, some of those arms have magnets at their tips. The hidden electronics can sense which arms move. From that pattern, the device can pick up much of what's being typed. Once it has enough data, it sends a short radio burst to a receiver nearby.

The designers give those bursts somewhere to hide. They use frequencies in the same band as Soviet television broadcasts. They can also switch the bugs off remotely. Some versions draw power straight from the typewriter. Others carry their own batteries.

That difference explains a missed chance six years earlier. In 1978, American inspectors had already X-rayed parts of the embassy's typewriters. They aimed at the motor and the power components, where they expected to see a modification. The bugs in use then ran on batteries. The parts they were looking at could appear perfectly normal.

There are gaps in what comes out. The space bar doesn't move the ball, so the bug misses the spaces between words. Some letters are hard to tell apart. To read the text, Soviet analysts have to work out where the words begin and end.

## Chapter Four · What Moscow Read  ·  217 words, about 104 s

The Americans eventually find sixteen altered typewriters, thirteen from the Moscow embassy and three from the consulate in Leningrad. The earliest had shipped in 1976. Investigators think Soviet intelligence probably got access while the machines were in transit. The State Department later learns that one typewriter was left in Soviet customs overnight.

Now the FBI has to work out who used the machines and what they might have typed. Some inventory records were routinely destroyed after two years. Staff rotated through posts. The trail back to individual desks is incomplete.

But a former KGB officer gives the NSA one example of what the Soviets got. A bugged machine had been used in the office of the American agricultural attaché. According to the defector, Soviet intelligence pulled out the American position on a grain deal and passed it to its negotiators before the next session.

In March 1985, CBS breaks the story. The State Department tightens procedures for shipping and inspecting equipment. The full list of documents intercepted stays beyond what U.S. investigators can reconstruct.

The embassy could encrypt a message before sending it to Washington. It could send the paper in a sealed pouch. By the time the page came out of a bugged machine, part of that message could already have reached a Soviet listening post.

# 4. They Paid $30 for Radioactive Scrap

## Intro  ·  67 words, about 32 s

In December 1998, two brothers in Istanbul pay about thirty dollars for a pair of old metal containers. They break them open to sell the metal. Within days, people who helped take them apart start vomiting. So do some of the people who just stood nearby.

The family suspects the lead inside the containers. They bury it.

The thing that's making them sick stays in the yard.

## Chapter One · Across the Street  ·  276 words, about 132 s

The brothers, Naki and İlyas Ilgaz, live in İkitelli, in western Istanbul. The containers come from a building that has just changed hands. The new owners are clearing it out. The brothers load both packages onto a truck and take them home.

On December tenth, the family starts taking them apart on a patch of open ground across from a relative's house. They strip away the outer cases and wooden crates. Then they pull the shielding plug and a sliding assembly out of one container. One man reaches into the opening to see what's inside.

Bad weather interrupts the work. They load everything back on the truck and roll it off into a yard beside the family home. The yard is open to the street. There are houses right next to it.

On December thirteenth, they go back to work. They open the second container and use a torch and an excavator to peel away the steel shells. Relatives watch as the lead inside is exposed.

By late afternoon, the people doing the work start feeling sick. Others who were nearby develop similar symptoms. At a local clinic, food poisoning and lead poisoning are both suspected. The patients get fluids. When the vomiting subsides, they go home.

A few days later, the man who reached inside notices that two fingertips are turning red.

Near the end of December, the scrap is loaded onto a truck by hand and with a shovel, then taken to a larger yard. By early January, much of that pile has already gone to a smelting works east of Istanbul. The capsule is still buried in the scrap at the second yard.

## Chapter Two · The Mark on the Container  ·  254 words, about 122 s

On January eighth, two of the men go to a larger hospital. Nearly four weeks have passed since people first fell ill. They're still weak. Some of the people who worked on the containers have started bleeding from their gums.

A doctor named Çağlar Canpolat asks what they've been working with. The men describe the lead containers. Canpolat considers why a container would be lined with so much lead in the first place. Then the son of one of the patients mentions a mark he saw on the container. Three blades around a central circle. A radiation warning.

Canpolat calls the local nuclear research center. By three that afternoon, specialists are at the entrance to the second scrapyard. They're already getting high readings. The workers are evacuated. Police seal off the area.

The containers were built to carry cobalt-sixty for cancer treatment. Inside a radiotherapy machine, a source like this sends a beam of gamma rays toward a tumor. Heavy shielding blocks much of the radiation in other directions. A hospital eventually replaces a source when it gets too weak for treatment. It can still give someone nearby a dangerous dose.

This one was supposed to go back to its supplier in the United States. The company got permission to export it in 1994. Instead, it kept the packages in Ankara. Two eventually went to a warehouse in Istanbul, then into the adjoining building. For months, they sat near the front, behind unlocked doors. When that building was sold, the packages went with it.

## Chapter Three · The Pile  ·  284 words, about 136 s

Now the team needs to find the capsule in a yard full of broken metal.

They spot a cylindrical part from one of the containers. It looks like the piece that could still be holding the source. The crew makes a shielded box with lead bricks, then practices lifting a piece of pipe with the scrapyard's mechanical grab.

The real lift works on the first try. The crew drops the cylinder into the box. The readings in the yard stay high. The cylinder they've just recovered is empty.

The detector leads them farther into the yard, to a heap of scrap. The grab lifts pieces away. The radiation readings go up. The pile itself has been shielding the capsule. The more metal they remove, the less protection they have.

They call off the search for the day.

On January tenth, a truck backs into the yard with a radiation detector on a long boom. A thick wall of concrete blocks shields the operator. A crane holds a lead-lined drum. Ten pairs of responders take turns. One person moves pieces of scrap with a long pole. The other shovels anything suspicious into the drum. The crane lifts it so the team can check what's inside.

Ten attempts. Still nothing.

The team considers burying the pile in concrete. That would shield whatever is in there, and they'd lose the chance to identify it. They check the radiation doses recorded by the workers and decide they can make another round of attempts.

On the fourteenth try, they recover the capsule. Its casing is intact. The cobalt has stayed sealed inside all this time. Gamma rays were able to reach people as soon as the shielding was removed.

## Chapter Four · The Second Container  ·  199 words, about 96 s

Eighteen people are admitted for observation and treatment. Ten adults are diagnosed with acute radiation sickness. The radiation has damaged the marrow that makes their blood cells. Doctors give some of them platelet transfusions to reduce the risk of bleeding. All ten survive.

By then, investigators have another question. The family bought two containers. Could both have held a source?

An international team retraces the route. They survey the yard beside the house, the second scrapyard and the smelting works, about ninety kilometers east of Istanbul. They find only normal background radiation. Then they compare serial numbers. On which sources were sent back to America and which stayed in Turkey, the company's records and the supplier's records contradict each other.

The investigators conclude that the second container was probably empty. They can't establish that for certain. After the accident, Turkey inspects all forty-six of its radiotherapy centers and tightens the rules for storing and returning used sources.

By the time the man who reached into the container leaves the hospital, the wounds on his fingers have healed. In November, a scan shows that blood is no longer reaching the tip of his index finger.

Surgeons have to remove it.

# 5. The $25 Million Hidden in a Drain

## Intro  ·  78 words, about 37 s

In March 2011, French police search a house outside Paris for the fifth time. This time, they notice a patch of concrete in a rainwater drain. It's a little lighter than the rest.

They drill through it. Underneath is a plastic container. Inside is a ring set with a thirty-one-carat diamond.

The stone came from Harry Winston, a jeweler near the Champs-Élysées. Armed men had robbed the same shop twice. The first time, they came dressed as painters.

## Chapter One · The Service Door  ·  109 words, about 52 s

On the morning of October sixth, 2007, staff are opening the Harry Winston boutique on Avenue Montaigne. Four men in painters' overalls are on the premises. They've used the service entrance. And they're armed.

The men threaten and tie up staff. They force a manager to deactivate alarms and open the safes. In minutes, they take a hundred and twenty watches and three hundred and sixty pieces of jewelry.

On the service stairs, the robbers empty fire extinguishers. According to Le Parisien's reconstruction, it's an attempt to cover their tracks. There are employees who can tell police what happened. There's still a question about how the men got inside.

## Chapter Two · The Front Door  ·  222 words, about 107 s

Fourteen months later, the boutique is open for business. It's the fourth of December, 2008. Christmas shoppers are out on the avenue. This time, four men approach the front door. Some are wearing wigs and women's clothes. A guard lets them in.

Upstairs, the disguises come off. The visitors pull handguns on two security staff. One of the men goes up another floor and orders staff and a customer downstairs. Soon, sixteen people are on the floor. Some of the employees are called by name.

The robbers have brought a suitcase. They make staff open display cases and safes. Some of the most valuable pieces are kept in concealed compartments. The men know where to look.

Before they leave, they show the people on the floor a grenade and threaten to booby-trap the exit. A driver picks them up outside. They're gone in less than twenty minutes. Court documents put the count at two hundred and ninety-seven pieces of jewelry and a hundred and four watches. At the trial, the second haul alone is valued at seventy-one million euros.

The insurer offers a million-dollar reward for information leading to the return of the jewels. Detectives have a question of their own. Who knew the shop well enough to tell the men where to go, what to open and whose name to call?

## Chapter Three · A Little Cement  ·  375 words, about 180 s

The case lands with the Paris police unit that investigates armed robberies. A tip from another police unit gives them names to work with. Among them is Douadi Yahiaoui, a man known as Doudou. Investigators are also looking at one of Harry Winston's guards, Mouloud Djennad.

A link between the two men runs through a gym. Djennad goes to the same one as Yahiaoui's brother-in-law, Patrick Chiniah. The court will later hear that Chiniah puts them in touch.

Detectives put microphones in Yahiaoui's car. He's careful on the phone. In the car, police hear him talk about carats and Rapaport, a price guide used in the diamond trade. Over months of surveillance, officers watch envelopes change hands through car windows. Le Parisien later reports that one lot of thirty-nine stones brings in more than two hundred thousand euros. The sales give detectives something to act on. If they wait too long, more of the stones could disappear into the trade.

In June 2009, police move in around the time of a deal involving buyers from Israel. They detain more than two dozen people in raids across Paris and its suburbs. One search turns up two pairs of earrings. At a house in Les Pavillons-sous-Bois, there's a hidden compartment beneath a staircase. Police find jewels there, some still on their original Harry Winston stands.

It's Yahiaoui's house. Detectives have a place to look and proof that stolen pieces have been kept there. One stone they've heard about in intercepted conversations is still missing. It's the big stone that keeps coming up on the recordings.

They keep coming back to the house. Le Parisien reports that in one intercepted conversation, Yahiaoui mentioned doing a little cement work. On the fifth search, the officers find the lighter patch in the drain.

The container holds nineteen rings and several earrings. The thirty-one-carat diamond is among them. French reports value that stone alone at about six million euros. The Associated Press puts the value of the whole cache at roughly twenty-five million dollars.

The drain gives the police a haul they can lay out and count. The case still has to account for two armed robberies, two sets of disguises, and a guard who was supposed to protect the shop.

## Chapter Four · The Night Before  ·  203 words, about 97 s

In February 2015, eight men go on trial in Paris. Mouloud Djennad admits supplying information to Yahiaoui that helped the robbers. Trial reporting says that in 2007 he let the men in the evening before the raid. They spent the night inside the premises. When staff arrived in the morning, the men were already there.

Yahiaoui acknowledges a role in handling stolen goods. He disputes that he was the mastermind. Prosecutor Sylvie Kachaner tells the court he recruited the robbers, gave instructions and took charge of selling the jewels. Farid Allou admits taking part in both robberies. Another defendant admits his involvement in the second. Two others dispute their involvement, although investigators found traces of their DNA in the boutique.

Before the court deliberates, Djennad tells it he's thinking of his former colleagues at Harry Winston. "I'm ashamed every day," he says.

On February twenty-seventh, the court convicts all eight men. Yahiaoui receives the longest sentence, fifteen years. Allou gets ten. Djennad is sentenced to five years, three of them suspended.

Police can account for the thirty-one-carat diamond down to the patch of concrete that hid it. When the sentences come down, hundreds of the pieces taken from Avenue Montaigne are still missing.

# 6. The Killer Was Still in the House

## Intro  ·  40 words, about 19 s

The woman who lived in this Denver house has moved away. Her husband was murdered inside. Now the neighbors are seeing lights in the windows again.

They call the police. Officers search the house.

They come out without finding anyone.

## Chapter One · The House on Moncrieff Place  ·  298 words, about 143 s

On October seventeenth, 1941, Philip Peters is seventy-three years old. He used to work for the railroad. He and his wife, Helen, have lived on West Moncrieff Place for more than thirty years. They belong to a local guitar club and sometimes give music lessons.

For the past few weeks, Philip has been coming home to an empty house. Helen is in the hospital with a broken hip. While she recovers, neighbors invite him over for dinner. Sometimes they send him home with enough for lunch the next day.

Then one evening, Philip doesn't turn up for dinner. The neighbors go over to check on him. They get into the house and find him beaten to death in a front bedroom.

The house doesn't look as if someone has torn through it looking for valuables. Local accounts describe doors and windows that were locked and no obvious sign of forced entry. An iron tool used to shake ash loose in a stove is part of the crime scene. There are signs of a struggle in more than one room. The detectives search the house and look into Philip's past. They still have no one they can charge.

When Helen gets out of the hospital, she goes back to the house. A friend moves in to help her. Then food goes missing. Things turn up out of place. The women report noises inside the house.

Helen's friend comes to believe the place is haunted and leaves. Helen eventually moves to Grand Junction to live with her son. The house on West Moncrieff is supposed to be empty now. People nearby still report sounds and lights. There are complaints about a smell, too.

Officers search the place again. The reports keep coming. Eventually, detectives start watching the house from outside.

## Chapter Two · The Closet  ·  225 words, about 108 s

On July thirtieth, 1942, detectives Roy Bloxom and Bill Jackson are watching the Peters house. They see a man inside and rush in. Once they're in the house, the man is out of sight.

Then there's a sound from upstairs. A detective later describes it as something like a key turning in a lock.

They go up. A closet door opens. Above them, a pair of legs is disappearing through a small opening in the ceiling.

The detectives grab hold and pull the man back down.

He's severely thin. His hair is long and his clothes are in tatters. He gives police a name. It takes more questioning before the captain identifies him as Theodore Coneys.

The police send an officer named Fred Zarnow up through the opening. The opening is reported to be about eight inches by fifteen. Beyond it, the roof slopes down on both sides. There's no room to stand.

There's a hot plate and a radio Coneys took from the cellar. He had rigged an electrical outlet to use them. He'd also been keeping his waste up there. The smell is so bad that Zarnow is sick.

The crawl space explains where the man went when the detectives entered. In questioning, Coneys explains how long he's been using it.

He says he'd moved in about five weeks before Philip was killed.

## Chapter Three · Five Weeks Earlier  ·  315 words, about 151 s

Coneys and the Peterses knew each other years earlier through the Denver Guitar Club. He'd been a guest in this same house. By 1941, he's back in Denver and short of money. He tells police he sees Philip leave one day in September and finds a door unlocked. Inside the house, he discovers the opening in the closet ceiling.

He starts coming down to take food. The Associated Press reports what Philip catches him doing on October seventeenth. He's at the icebox, tearing at a roast.

After Coneys confesses, detectives bring him back to the house to walk them through the attack. He takes them through the kitchen, toward the front door and into the front bedroom. In his account, that's where the struggle ends. He strikes Philip with the heavy iron stove tool.

When the neighbors come looking for Philip, Coneys has already climbed back into the crawl space. According to his account, he's up there while the investigation begins below him.

As the weeks pass, Helen comes home, then moves away. Coneys stays. A local reconstruction based on newspaper coverage describes him taking food from the cellar, including jars of preserves. He stays through the winter and into the following summer. By the time police pull him down, more than nine months have passed since the killing.

Newspapers call him the Denver Spider Man. The prosecution asks for the death penalty. Coneys takes the stand. He admits beating Philip to death and argues that he was defending himself. On October thirty-first, 1942, the jury deliberates for less than two hours, finds him guilty of first-degree murder and gives him life in prison.

He'll spend the rest of his life in the state penitentiary in Cañon City, where he dies in 1967. The Associated Press reports what Coneys says about the sentence.

"I'll have a better home than I have had in many years."

# 7. The Kidnapper Who Only Played a Radio Jingle

The Kidnapper Who Only Played a Radio Jingle
Narration: 2,362 words · Estimated finished runtime: about 13 minutes and 10 seconds at 184 words per minute, with 20 seconds of pauses.

Cold Open
The caller plays a radio jingle. Ursula Herrmann's mother asks for the names her daughter gave two of her stuffed toys. She's agreed to pay two million marks. All she wants now is some sign that her ten-year-old is alive.
The person on the other end says nothing.
Police are recording the call. Between the notes and the silence, the tape picks up a few faint clicks. Those clicks will help send a man to prison. And one of the people who will challenge that evidence is Ursula's own brother.

Chapter One · Ten Minutes Home
Two days before the first call, Ursula sits down at a piano in her family's house in Eching, a village on Bavaria's Ammersee. Her older brother Michael helps her practice. It's the first day of the school year. She still has gymnastics that afternoon.
Ursula takes her red bicycle down the lakeside path to Schondorf. She knows the route. After gymnastics, she stops for dinner at her cousin's house. At about seven twenty, her mother calls to say it's time to come home. The ride takes about ten minutes.
Half an hour later, her mother calls again. Ursula's aunt says she left twenty-five minutes ago.
Her father heads down the path from Eching. Her uncle comes from the other direction. Somewhere between the two villages, the men meet. They call Ursula's name into the trees. By the time police, firefighters, and neighbors are out looking, it's dark.
Close to midnight, a search dog leads its handler away from the lakeshore. In the brush, about twenty meters off the path, there's a red bicycle. Ursula is gone.
Michael is playing music at a friend's house when he learns that Ursula is missing. He comes home and joins the search. Earlier that day, his sister had been beside him at the piano. Now the searchers have a bicycle to show where she got to.
By morning, a helicopter is up. Police search the shore, and divers check the shallows. The radio carries a description of a missing girl in a gray cardigan and dark green trousers. The following morning, a sound from that same medium reaches her parents in a very different way.
The telephone rings. There's a short melody they recognize. It's what Bayern Three plays around its traffic bulletins. The line goes quiet. The melody plays again. Then the caller hangs up.
More calls follow. They make sense only when an envelope arrives for Ursula's father on Friday. Inside, bits of newspaper have been cut out and assembled into sentences. The writer says they've taken his daughter. They want two million marks in used hundred-mark notes. The family is supposed to hear a signal on the phone and give a simple answer. Will they pay?
The letter says to expect the signal on Thursday. Thursday has already passed. The court will later hear that the envelopes were missing the postal code, which delayed delivery. For hours, Ursula's parents had been hearing a signal whose instructions were still in the mail.
When the caller tries again, Ursula's mother says they'll pay. She asks about the stuffed toys. The line gives her nothing she can use to tell whether her daughter is safe.
A second letter acknowledges that the family has agreed. It gives Ursula's father instructions for the handoff. A suitcase full of used notes. A yellow Fiat Six Hundred. He must drive alone, stay below ninety kilometers an hour, and wait for a call telling him where to go. The letter promises to release Ursula six hours after the money is handed over.
A neighbor helps raise part of the ransom. The state agrees to cover the rest. The family can pay. They still need a destination.
The call giving them one never comes.

Chapter Two · Seven Bolts
Police go back into the forest. This time, they divide it into grids. More than a hundred officers and ten search dogs work through the sections. The officers use metal rods to check beneath the surface.
On October fourth, nineteen days after Ursula disappears, one of those rods hits something solid. The spot is in a small clearing, roughly eight hundred meters from where investigators believe she was taken. The officers clear the soil and uncover a wooden cover. Beneath it is a lid held shut by seven sliding bolts.
They force it open. Ursula is inside. She's dead.
The box is about the height of a ten-year-old child. It has a shelf, a seat with a toilet opening, and a light wired to a car battery buried outside. Inside are water, orange soda, chocolate, and books. There's even a portable radio. It's tuned to Bayern Three.
The builders have fitted two lengths of plastic pipe, one intended to bring in fresh air and one to let air out. They've drilled thousands of little holes in the pipes and added fabric to muffle sound. They've provided enough food and drink to keep a child there for days. In the forensic reconstruction, the ventilation barely exchanges air with the outside.
The medical estimate is that Ursula survives between thirty minutes and five hours after she's put inside. The experts can't pin it down further. They also can't establish whether she was sedated before she went into the box.
By the time her mother asks about the toys, Ursula is already dead.
The box weighs about sixty kilos. The covering goes under a layer of earth. The court's reconstruction describes five small spruces pushed into the ground over it. The top has a capped tube that the court believes was meant for talking to whoever was inside. The judges will later infer that the caller went back to the box after Ursula's mother asked for proof of life and discovered she was dead. That is the court's explanation for why the calls stopped.
Someone had to build the box, get it into the forest, dig deep enough to bury it upright, and disguise the spot. Whoever planned this had time to work. There's evidence of preparation back by the path, too.

Chapter Three · The Man with the Spade
Investigators eventually trace about a hundred and forty meters of green wire through the trees near the place Ursula's bicycle was found. The path bends sharply. The wire runs past the bend, connecting two positions with views in opposite directions. Investigators believe it could have let one person signal to another when somebody was coming. How it was used that evening remains uncertain.
Forensic investigators lift a fingerprint from adhesive tape on the box. As the inquiry expands, they will compare around twenty thousand fingerprints. They find no match for the print on the tape.
One name comes up within days of the discovery. Werner Mazurek lives a few hundred meters from the Herrmanns. He's a trained mechanic with a television repair business and substantial debts. When police ask where he was the night Ursula vanished, he eventually says he was playing the board game Risk with his wife and two friends. An early search of his home and workshop turns up nothing that ties him to the crime.
Then police speak to Klaus Pfaffinger, an acquaintance of Mazurek's. His landlord has reported seeing him with a spade strapped to his moped before the kidnapping.
Under questioning, Pfaffinger tells the detectives that Mazurek offered him a thousand marks and a color television to dig a hole in the woods. He says he dug it and later saw a box in it. Some of his details about the size of the hole and the soil will matter decades later.
First, the detectives take him into the woods and ask him to show them the spot. He fails to lead them to it. Back at the police station, he retracts his story. He never repeats it in later interrogations. Police release him without charge.
The investigation goes nationwide. A television appeal puts the case in front of viewers across West Germany. The tips keep coming. Eventually, the files on the leads fill twelve steel cabinets. By the end of the eighties, the investigation has slowed. The calls are still on tape.

Chapter Four · The Clicks
In the mid-2000s, investigators go back to the physical evidence. This time, they have DNA profiling. They recover several genetic profiles from traces on the box. In 2007, one from a screw appears to match a sample from a drinking glass in the apartment of a woman killed in Munich. It looks like a connection between two crimes. The man ultimately convicted in Munich was a small child in 1981. After further forensic work, the court in the Munich case finds no link between the two crimes.
By 2007, Pfaffinger is dead. Mazurek has moved to northern Germany and sells boat accessories. Investigators are running up against a deadline. The offense they're pursuing, kidnapping for ransom resulting in death, carries a thirty-year limitation period. A case that's already taken more than twenty-five years has only a few years left to bring charges.
Police put Mazurek under surveillance and search his home. They take a saliva sample. His DNA doesn't match the profiles recovered from the box. But police also take away something he says he bought at a flea market just weeks earlier.
A reel-to-reel tape recorder. A Grundig TK Two Forty-Eight.
A forensic phonetics specialist from the Bavarian state criminal police listens to the old calls. Of the recordings she examines, three contain the radio jingle. The station broadcast it to anyone with a receiver. She focuses on details a listener might miss, including the sounds around the melody and the way one of the highest notes drops in volume.
She tests the Grundig. Its controls produce noises that resemble some of the sounds on the calls. The comparison includes the play button, the pause button, and the way the machine sounds when its pause mechanism is operated remotely. Investigators test eleven comparable recorders. The seized machine produces the combination the expert finds most persuasive. Her conclusion is that it was probably one of the devices used to prepare the ransom recordings.
Police arrest Mazurek in May 2008. In court, his explanation is still the flea market. He says the Grundig came into his possession in 2007. Sellers questioned about the market can't recall the sale of the machine. The judges eventually reject his account and conclude he had it at the time of the kidnapping.
Ursula's brother Michael joins the criminal proceedings on behalf of the family. He's a music teacher now. He's also worked with recording equipment. He asks to see the files and reads thousands of pages in the first weeks of the trial. Somewhere in that paperwork is the detail that he helped Ursula practice piano the day she disappeared. He'd forgotten that moment until he read about it in the file.
The sound evidence bothers him. Start with the radio broadcast. In the court's reconstruction, someone records and edits the jingle on the Grundig. That version is copied onto a smaller player. The player goes to a public phone booth, where its speaker plays into the mouthpiece. The signal travels down the phone line and is recorded at the Herrmanns' end.
If you hear a click on the police tape, where did it enter the chain? At the Grundig? At the second machine? In the booth? Even the change in volume matters. A note that comes out quieter at the end could have lost some of its strength at an earlier step.
The sound on the police tape has passed through every one of those steps. The portable player, the booth, and the telephone line can all change it. Michael wants to know how confidently the expert can work backward from that recording and identify one machine at the start. He puts his objections in writing to the court.

Chapter Five · After the Verdict
The judges weigh the tape alongside the rest of the evidence. They credit details in Pfaffinger's original statement despite his retraction. They conclude that a pair of binoculars found at the abduction site belonged to Mazurek. They consider his debts, his workshop skills, and the case for his having owned the Grundig in 1981.
In March 2010, after fifty-six days of hearings, the court finds Werner Mazurek guilty of kidnapping for ransom resulting in death and sentences him to life in prison. His wife, who was tried as an accessory, is acquitted. Mazurek maintains his innocence. Germany's Federal Court of Justice upholds the conviction.
Michael finds another way to put parts of the case back in front of judges.
In 2013, he sues Mazurek for twenty thousand euros. Michael has developed tinnitus, a persistent noise in his ear that interferes with teaching music and sleeping. A medical assessment links the condition to the stress of the criminal proceedings. For Michael, the civil claim is also a chance to get the evidence examined again.
A retired physicist named Bernd Haider borrows a comparable recorder and spends about a year testing it against the call recordings. He says he can't reproduce the state expert's findings and offers his work to the defense.
The civil court initially awards Michael seven thousand euros. In reaching that decision, it agrees with the criminal court about who kidnapped his sister. Michael also submits material to prosecutors raising a possible lead involving a boarding school near the woods. In 2019, prosecutors say the material gives them no grounds to reopen the case.
The award is overturned on appeal in 2020. The appeals court finds that Michael's illness arose too long after the crime to qualify for damages on that basis. The criminal verdict stands.
In June 2023, after more than fifteen years in custody, Mazurek is released on probation. He continues to deny involvement. In 2024, German public broadcasters revisit the case, following the defense's effort to clear him and examining the alternative leads around the boarding school. The conviction remains in force.
A court has ruled on who took Ursula. The recording preserves what her mother was trying to find out before anyone knew where to look. After agreeing to pay, she asked the caller for something that would have cost him nothing to give.
Two names. Something her daughter would know.
On the other end of the line, nobody answered.
