## COLD OPEN

At seven in the morning on June the twelfth, 1962, a guard walked down cell block B on Alcatraz
Island, counting heads.

Every man was in bed. Every face was on its pillow.

But in three of those cells, the faces weren't faces.

They were made of soap, toilet paper and concrete dust, painted skin-coloured, with real human
hair glued on top.

The men who made them had been gone for nine hours.

And sixty years later, nobody can tell you for sure where they went.

*[cold open: corridor at dawn, the guard's flashlight, a slow push onto the dummy head, a hard cut to the title]*

## CHAPTER 1: THE ROCK

Alcatraz was built to be the end of the line.

A federal prison on a rock in the middle of San Francisco Bay, surrounded by freezing water and
currents strong enough to drag a swimmer out under the Golden Gate.

The city was right there. You could see the lights at night. Sometimes, on New Year's Eve, prisoners said
you could hear the parties drifting across the water.

That was part of the punishment.

Over the years, more than thirty men tried to escape. Most were caught. Some were shot. A few
went into the water and were never seen again.

As far as the government was concerned, nobody had ever made it off the Rock.

*[the island at night in fog, the lighthouse beam, the city lights across the water, a map of the bay]*

## CHAPTER 2: THE MEN

Frank Lee Morris arrived in January 1960. He was a lifelong escape artist, an orphan
who had been in and out of institutions since he was a boy, and he was smart. Very smart.

A few months later, two brothers from a farming family in Florida joined him: John and Clarence
Anglin, bank robbers who had already broken out of other prisons.

And in the cell next to Morris was a fourth man, Allen West.

Together, they started working on a plan. It took them about six months.

*[the evidence board: four mugshots pinned up, red string linking them; the hero in his detective coat]*

## CHAPTER 3: THE VENTS

At the back of every cell, under the sink, was a small air vent, set into old concrete
that the salty sea air had been eating away for decades.

So they started digging.

With spoons stolen from the mess hall. With pieces of saw blade. And later with a homemade
drill they built from the motor of a broken vacuum cleaner.

The noise was a problem. So during the prison's music hour, Morris played his accordion, and
under the music, the others drilled.

Every night they hid the holes behind fake vent covers, painted to look like the real thing.

Behind the vents was a dark utility corridor full of pipes. And above it, the empty top level of
the cell block, where nobody ever went.

That became their workshop.

*[the cell at night: digging with a spoon, dust falling; the accordion; the fake grille slid back into place]*

## CHAPTER 4: THE WORKSHOP

Up there in the dark, they built everything they would need.

Life vests, and a raft about six feet wide and fourteen feet long, made from more than fifty
prison raincoats, stitched and sealed so they wouldn't leak. Morris had found the idea in a magazine,
*Popular Mechanics*.

Paddles, cut from plywood.

And to blow it all up fast, Morris's own concertina, turned into a bellows.

Then came the final piece: the heads.

Soap, toothpaste, concrete dust and toilet paper, shaped into faces, painted, and finished with
real hair taken from the prison barber shop.

From the hallway, in the dark, they looked exactly like sleeping men.

*[the workshop: raincoats laid out, the raft taking shape, a dummy head being painted under one bulb]*

## CHAPTER 5: THE NIGHT

June the eleventh, 1962. Lights out at nine thirty.

Morris and the Anglins put the dummy heads in their beds, pulled the blankets up, and slipped
through the vents.

But Allen West couldn't get out. The cement around his vent had hardened, and by the time he'd
broken through, the others were gone.

The three men climbed up through the pipes to the roof. They had already worked loose the rivets on
a big ventilator fan, and they squeezed out into the night wind.

Then they crossed the roof, slid about fifty feet down a kitchen vent pipe to the ground,
climbed two twelve-foot barbed-wire fences, and reached the northeast shore of the island, a
blind spot the searchlights didn't cover.

They pumped up the raft with the concertina.

And at around ten o'clock, three men pushed off into the black water of San Francisco Bay.

*[a slow creep along the roof, the fan, the long slide down the pipe, the fences; the raft in the fog, the beam sweeping past]*

## CHAPTER 6: THE MORNING

Nobody noticed for nine hours.

At the seven a.m. count, the guards finally realised the faces on the pillows weren't real.

Alarms. Boats. Helicopters. Soldiers searching the shorelines. The manhunt lasted about ten days.

Two days after the escape, the Coast Guard pulled a homemade paddle out of the water near Angel
Island. A packet turned up with personal photos belonging to the Anglin family.

Pieces of homemade life vest washed up on the beaches.

But the raft was never found.

And neither were the men.

*[the manhunt: boats and searchlights; the map with the found items appearing one by one; the stamped case file]*

## CHAPTER 7: THE THEORIES

The FBI investigated for seventeen years. In 1979 they closed the file with their conclusion: the
three men most likely drowned in the cold water.

But not everyone is convinced.

In 2003, the TV show *MythBusters* built a copy of the raincoat raft and made the crossing. Their
verdict: possible.

In 2014, scientists in the Netherlands modelled the tides of that night. They found that if the men had left
at around eleven thirty, the current could have carried them to land near the Golden Gate
Bridge. A little earlier or later, and the bay would probably have swept them out to sea.

In 2013, the San Francisco police received a letter. The writer claimed to be John Anglin. *"We all
made it that night,"* it said, *"but barely."* The FBI couldn't prove it was him, and couldn't prove it wasn't.

And the Anglin family has shown a photo they say was taken in Brazil in 1975: two men who
look a lot like the brothers.

Alcatraz itself closed less than a year after the escape. It was too old and too expensive to keep running.

But the case is still open. The U.S. Marshals are still looking.

*[the evidence board fills up: the raft, the tide map, the letter, the photo; the strings all lead to one big question mark]*

## ENDING

So, did three men really beat the most famous prison in America, then vanish into
a new life?

Or did the bay take them that night, the way it had taken so many before?

Look at the evidence. And tell me in the comments:

**Did they make it?**

*[the slow pull back from the board to the empty raft on dark water; fade; end screen]*
