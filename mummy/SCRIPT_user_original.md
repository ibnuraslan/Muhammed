The Mummy Made From Three Different People
1,803 narration words · Approximately 12–13 minutes

Beneath a house in Scotland, archaeologists uncover a body with its knees drawn up against its chest.
Someone has carefully placed it in the ground.
But the man’s teeth don’t make sense.
The skull has lost its back teeth long before death. The lower jaw still has teeth that were being used.
They belong to different people.
Then the rest of the skeleton is examined. The body belongs to a third man.
Three people have been arranged to look like one. And in the same house, there is another body.
It’s 2001. Archaeologist Mike Parker Pearson and his team are excavating Cladh Hallan, on the island of South Uist, off Scotland’s western coast.
Sand has buried layers of prehistoric life here. Dig through them and the team can follow what happened to the same patch of ground over centuries. Farmers ploughed it. People buried their dead in it. Later, they built houses over it.
Three roundhouses sit in a row, sharing walls. Their entrances face east. Inside, there are hearths, storage pits, and the debris of cooking and making things. Floor after floor has survived beneath the sand.
Under the northern house are the two adults.
The bones have been laid out in the shape of bodies, tightly curled. At first, each looks like one person. The lower jaw changes that.
The owner of the skull lost his upper chewing teeth years before he died. The bone around those empty sockets has healed. But the teeth in the lower jaw show that they were still meeting teeth above them.
That jaw had a different skull when its owner was alive.
Chemical analysis adds another mismatch. The skull and jaw have a different isotopic signature from the leg bone. Together with the physical evidence, the tests reveal parts from three men.
Whoever buried them has put the head of one man above the body of another, and given that head someone else’s jaw.
To find out what happened, the team has to establish when these men died.
Radiocarbon dating gives them a problem. The parts don’t all fit neatly into the same period. Some may be separated by generations. And the houses above them were built later still, around the eleventh century BC.
The dates come with ranges. They can’t give each person a precise year of death. But the remains have a history that stretches back well before the house.
For a body to be kept that long, something has to happen to it.
The bones are still arranged as if the joints were held together when the body went into the ground. Yet a corpse left to decay normally would lose the tissue holding those joints in place.
The team looks inside the bones.
Under a microscope, decomposition leaves damage of its own. Bacteria can spread through a decaying body and create tiny tunnels in bone. Those changes can survive long after the flesh has gone.
In the Cladh Hallan adults, that damage is unusually limited. Decay appears to have begun, then been interrupted. Animal bones from the same sandy ground show much more bacterial damage.
The researchers conclude that these people had once been mummified.
By the time archaeologists find them, the skin and other soft tissues have disappeared. The evidence of preservation survives inside the skeletons.
There’s another trace on the outside. The surfaces have lost some of their minerals through exposure to acid. The sand where the team finds the bodies is rich in shell fragments. It doesn’t explain that earlier treatment.
Elsewhere on South Uist, peat bogs offer an environment that could.
A body placed in suitable bog conditions can resist decomposition. The researchers propose that the islanders put the dead into peat for a limited time, then retrieved them. Long enough to preserve tissue. Short enough for the bones to remain intact.
Nobody has found a written instruction or the place where they did it. The bones record the result. People on this island appear to have worked out how to preserve a corpse more than three thousand years ago.
And at some point, preserved parts from different corpses were brought together.
The second adult now demands a closer look.
Its pelvis is identified as female. Its head is identified as male. The head also appears to be older than the torso, potentially by several generations.
Researchers take samples from four places. The skull. The lower jaw. The upper arm. The thigh.
Ancient DNA is damaged and difficult to recover. Modern DNA can get onto old bones during handling, so the team has to separate likely contamination from sequences that could belong to the people buried here.
The results are published in 2012.
The jaw, upper arm, and thigh come from different individuals. The skull doesn’t yield enough reliable DNA for a conclusion of its own.
Physical analysis has already identified a mismatched head and torso. Now genetics establishes that this second body also contains remains from at least three people.
Under one house, the team has found two assembled bodies containing parts from at least six individuals.
But those six lives still don’t explain how the bodies ended up there.
The second skeleton is missing part of its left knee. The break is below the other leg, which lies across it. Whoever placed the body in the grave arranged the right leg over a left leg that was already incomplete.
That helps establish an order. The knee went missing before this burial. Taking it out afterward would have disturbed the bones above it.
Outside the house, in a separate pit, excavators find pieces of a human knee.
The broken surfaces match.
The missing part of the body has been buried about five meters away.
The fractures tell the team something else. These bones broke after they had lost much of the collagen that gives fresh bone its flexibility. By then, the person had been dead for a considerable time.
But parts of the broken joint are still together.
The evidence points toward an old body that retained enough tissue to hold its bones in place, even while the bones themselves had become brittle.
In the team’s later reconstruction, that pit may have been an earlier grave. Someone dug it open and lifted out a preserved body. The knee broke and stayed behind.
The rest was moved beneath the house.
This changes the picture of what might have happened. The remains could have been preserved, buried, retrieved, altered, and buried again. The head and arm could have been substituted during that process.
The team cannot recover every step. It can connect the broken knee to the body and establish that the separation happened before the final burial. The precise moment when each different person became part of the whole remains uncertain.
And the knee is part of a much wider pattern.
Before the roundhouses existed, this ground held a cemetery. Some people were cremated. Small quantities of their burnt bones went into the earth.
Later, someone dug down into those graves.
In one case, a house had been built with its hearth directly over a child’s cremation burial. After that house was taken down, a pit was dug through its old floor to reach the remains.
People were returning to the dead across generations. Sometimes they removed bones. Sometimes they moved them. Human remains also ended up within the later houses’ walls and floors.
The two assembled bodies belong to a place where the dead repeatedly came back into the lives of the people building above them.
So why combine them?
One possibility is that the bodies represented ancestors. A skull, an arm, and a torso from different people could bring several lines of descent into one figure.
If ancestry helped establish who belonged to a family or who could claim a place, such a body might have carried authority. Researchers have suggested that preserving the dead could also have marked status.
Those explanations fit a community carefully handling old remains. But the bones cannot tell us what the people called these figures or what they believed the figures could do.
Even the relationships between the original people remain uncertain. Different DNA sequences establish that bones came from different individuals. They don’t, by themselves, prove that those people were unrelated.
There could have been family connections the available tests cannot reconstruct.
Another possibility is more practical. Preserved bodies could become damaged. Parts might be replaced as the remains were handled, moved, or retrieved from graves. The broken knee shows how easily an old body could come apart.
The two explanations could overlap. Repairing an ancestor might also change whom that ancestor represented.
The final arrangement is what survives. Someone assembled the parts into bodies, lowered them into graves, and built above them.
The houses continued to change. Floors were renewed. Rooms were used for cooking, sleeping, storage, and craft work. In the largest house, occupation lasted for centuries.
Above the buried remains, people ground grain, worked materials, and raised new floors over old ones. Their own descendants changed the buildings again.
By the time sand covered the settlement, the people who knew the bodies’ histories were gone.
The excavation brings those bodies back into view. Each test separates something the ancient arrangement had joined. Teeth distinguish one man’s jaw from another man’s skull. Chemistry separates the head from the body. DNA reveals a third person within the second skeleton.
Then the broken knee reconnects a body to a place outside the house where it had been before.
The discovery also gives researchers a way to look for something they could previously have missed.
A skeleton in a museum collection might once have been a mummy. If the skin has disappeared, the preservation could still be visible under a microscope.
In a study published in 2015, researchers examine bone samples from across Britain. Sixteen of the thirty-six Bronze Age individuals they study show unusually limited bacterial damage, consistent with earlier preservation.
It suggests the practice extended beyond this island. Some communities may have dried their dead. Others may have used wetlands.
The implication reaches into collections already excavated. Bodies recorded simply as skeletons may have had a longer history before burial. Cladh Hallan has given archaeologists a way to begin finding them.
There are still missing pieces. The remaining bones of the six people have not been accounted for. Their names are gone. Nobody knows why these particular parts were selected.
What survives is the result of someone’s decisions.
A skull was kept. A jaw was chosen. An arm was moved. Old remains were handled carefully enough to be arranged into bodies and buried beneath a place where people would live.
More than three thousand years later, they still look like individual people.
The archaeologists uncovered two bodies. The people who made them had buried at least six.
