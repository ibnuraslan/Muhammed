The houses continued to change. Floors were renewed. Rooms were used for cooking, sleeping, storage, and craft work. In the largest house, occupation lasted for centuries.

Above the buried remains, people ground grain, worked materials, and raised new floors over old ones. Their own descendants changed the buildings again.

By the time sand covered the settlement, the people who knew the bodies’ histories were gone.

The excavation brings those bodies back into view. Each test separates something the ancient arrangement had joined. Teeth distinguish one man’s jaw from another man’s skull. Chemistry separates the head from the body. DNA reveals a third person within the second skeleton.

Then the broken knee reconnects a body to a place outside the house where it had been before.

The discovery also gives researchers a way to look for something they could previously have missed.

A skeleton in a museum collection might once have been a mummy. If the skin has disappeared, the preservation could still be visible under a microscope.

In a study published in 2015, researchers examine bone samples from across Britain. Sixteen of the thirty-six Bronze Age individuals they study show unusually limited bacterial damage, consistent with earlier preservation.

It suggests the practice extended beyond this island. Some communities may have dried their dead. Others may have used wetlands.

The implication reaches into collections already excavated. Bodies recorded simply as skeletons may have had a longer history before burial. Cladh Hallan has given archaeologists a way to begin finding them.

There are still missing pieces. The remaining bones of the six people have not been accounted for. Their names are gone. Nobody knows why these particular parts were selected.

What survives is the result of someone’s decisions.

A skull was kept. A jaw was chosen. An arm was moved. Old remains were handled carefully enough to be arranged into bodies and buried beneath a place where people would live.

More than three thousand years later, they still look like individual people.

The archaeologists uncovered two bodies. The people who made them had buried at least six.
