This changes the picture of what might have happened. The remains could have been preserved, buried, retrieved, altered, and buried again. The head and arm could have been substituted during that process.

The team cannot recover every step. It can connect the broken knee to the body and establish that the separation happened before the final burial. The precise moment when each different person became part of the whole remains uncertain.

And the knee is part of a much wider pattern.

Before the roundhouses existed, this ground held a cemetery. Some people were cremated. Small quantities of their burnt bones went into the earth.

Later, someone dug down into those graves.

In one case, a house had been built with its hearth directly over a child’s cremation burial. After that house was taken down, a pit was dug through its old floor to reach the remains.

People were returning to the dead across generations. Sometimes they removed bones. Sometimes they moved them. Human remains also ended up within the later houses’ walls and floors.

The two assembled bodies belong to a place where the dead repeatedly came back into the lives of the people building above them.

So why combine them?

One possibility is that the bodies represented ancestors. A skull, an arm, and a torso from different people could bring several lines of descent into one figure.

If ancestry helped establish who belonged to a family or who could claim a place, such a body might have carried authority. Researchers have suggested that preserving the dead could also have marked status.

Those explanations fit a community carefully handling old remains. But the bones cannot tell us what the people called these figures or what they believed the figures could do.

Even the relationships between the original people remain uncertain. Different DNA sequences establish that bones came from different individuals. They don’t, by themselves, prove that those people were unrelated.

There could have been family connections the available tests cannot reconstruct.

Another possibility is more practical. Preserved bodies could become damaged. Parts might be replaced as the remains were handled, moved, or retrieved from graves. The broken knee shows how easily an old body could come apart.

The two explanations could overlap. Repairing an ancestor might also change whom that ancestor represented.

The final arrangement is what survives. Someone assembled the parts into bodies, lowered them into graves, and built above them.
