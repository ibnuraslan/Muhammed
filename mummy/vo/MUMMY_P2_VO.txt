To find out what happened, the team has to establish when these men died.

Radiocarbon dating gives them a problem. The parts don’t all fit neatly into the same period. Some may be separated by generations. And the houses above them were built later still, around the eleventh century BC.

The dates come with ranges. They can’t give each person a precise year of death. But the remains have a history that stretches back well before the house.

For a body to be kept that long, something has to happen to it.

The bones are still arranged as if the joints were held together when the body went into the ground. Yet a corpse left to decay normally would lose the tissue holding those joints in place.

The team looks inside the bones.

Under a microscope, decomposition leaves damage of its own. Bacteria can spread through a decaying body and create tiny tunnels in bone. Those changes can survive long after the flesh has gone.

In the Cladh Hallan adults, that damage is unusually limited. Decay appears to have begun, then been interrupted. Animal bones from the same sandy ground show much more bacterial damage.

The researchers conclude that these people had once been mummified.

By the time archaeologists find them, the skin and other soft tissues have disappeared. The evidence of preservation survives inside the skeletons.

There’s another trace on the outside. The surfaces have lost some of their minerals through exposure to acid. The sand where the team finds the bodies is rich in shell fragments. It doesn’t explain that earlier treatment.

Elsewhere on South Uist, peat bogs offer an environment that could.

A body placed in suitable bog conditions can resist decomposition. The researchers propose that the islanders put the dead into peat for a limited time, then retrieved them. Long enough to preserve tissue. Short enough for the bones to remain intact.

Nobody has found a written instruction or the place where they did it. The bones record the result. People on this island appear to have worked out how to preserve a corpse more than three thousand years ago.
